The 60 · boot sequence · golden run · 1080 × 1080 round

Three blinks,
a long wait,
then a pulse

Three rapid red blinks, then two full seconds of black — long enough that you start to wonder. An ember appears at the centre and grows without stopping into a circular bloom that runs out to the rim, where the whole edge becomes one line. That line releases and collapses back to the middle, and from there it beats: two sounds close together, then a rest, then again, the way a heart does.

Rendered at the panel's native 1080 × 1080 and displayed smaller, so point density and gradient banding are honest. 2,600 points on a golden-angle packing, lit strictly outward. 15.4 s end to end at 1× — the timing that used to be ¼× is now the design.

    The sequence as stills

    Six moments, held. The two-second black between the last blink and the ember has no still worth showing, which is the whole point of it.

    One event, three times

    The run, the collapse and every heartbeat are the same thing: a front — a radius, a softness and an amplitude — sweeping through the phyllotaxis field while points flare as it passes. Several are alive at once and each point sums them. That is why the beats look like the spiral run: they are the spiral run, fired again on a cardiac rhythm.

    The runOne front, out from the ember over 4.0 s, accelerating as rim · p^1.42. It does not stop at the rim, it keeps travelling and slides off the edge, so nothing switches from "running" to "lit".
    The collapseThe same front travelling the other way, rim to centre over 1.15 s, accelerating as it converges the way a converging wave does. It arrives at the centre exactly as the first heartbeat leaves it.
    Each beatTwo fronts. A strong one, then a weaker one at 52% amplitude 0.23 s behind it — lub-dub — then rest until the next cycle. Period 1.30 s, about 46 beats a minute. Each front crosses in 1.15 s and fades as it goes, so it is faint by the time it nears the rim and gone before it gets there.
    OverlapThree cycles are kept alive, so a beat launches while the previous one is still travelling. Up to six fronts can be summing at once.
    DecayBeat amplitude falls to about 62% over the first four beats, so it calms down without ever stopping.
    The rimAnswers the beat with a 0.55 s delay — it brightens when a wave gets near it, not when the wave leaves the centre.
    What is left of the bloomA small core glow at the middle, the source the fronts leave from. Everything else on screen after the rim strike is the point field.

    How the field is made organic

    Front softnessTight at the centre (16 px) and widening toward the rim (78 px). That is what makes the ember grow continuously into the field instead of a scatter popping into existence around it, and it applies to every front, not just the run.
    Granular ignitionEach point's effective radius is offset by a hash of its index, up to ±10 px, so points catch slightly early or late. Fronts stay circular; only their edges are grainy.
    Uneven flareSummed flare is scaled by a per-point hash, so some points catch hard and some barely — the same points every time, which gives the field a fixed texture.
    WanderEvery point drifts a couple of pixels on its own slow phase, which is what stops 2,600 points reading as a texture map.
    Nothing is strokedThe rim hairline is the only circle drawn as a circle. Every wave is points lighting up.

    What this costs on the panel

    Fill rate1080 × 1080 is 1.17 megapixels. Blitting a full frame at 60 fps is 70 Mpx/s before a single point is computed. A 2,600-point additive field is well past a software renderer on an ESP32-P4.
    FramebufferRGB565 at 1080 × 1080 is 2.33 MB a buffer, so double-buffered is about 4.7 MB of PSRAM before any asset.
    Now it is all one fieldMaking the beats look like the run means the heartbeat is a particle field too, so it cannot be a swept radial profile any more. It also has to run indefinitely while the device is still working, which rules out baked frames. The whole thing has to be computed.
    What that needsPer frame: 2,600 points, each summing up to six Gaussians, then one blit. Roughly 16,000 exponentials and 2,600 alpha-blended sprite draws. That is a GPU job or a much smaller point count — the honest options are dropping to about 700 points, rendering at 540 × 540 and upscaling, or both. Worth testing on the real board before the design is locked.
    Cheap winsThe Gaussian is a lookup table. The six-front sum is already reject-tested on a radius window, so most points touch one or two fronts. Point radii and the golden-angle positions are constant and can live in flash rather than being recomputed.
    SpritesThe same trick this page uses. The colour ramp is pre-baked into 14 gradient discs and every point is one blit. That is the shape the firmware wants too.
