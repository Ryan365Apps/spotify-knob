The Halo Section

The band is a display, not a mood light. Two changes make it read as one seamless line: point the LEDs somewhere other than at the diffuser, and give the diffuser enough thickness to survive being a part.

Three answers

Down beats up, and for the reason you gave. Firing up still leaves a direct sight line from each LED to the bottom of the diffuser's inner face, so the band would be brightest and dottiest along its lower edge — a gradient with the scallops strongest exactly where the eye lands. Fire down at a white floor and nothing the viewer sees is direct light. The floor becomes the source, and a lit patch on the floor is roughly twice as wide as the throw — an LED 4 mm up makes an 8 mm pool. That pool, not the 2 mm LED, is what the diffuser sees.

That is the prize, and it changes the count. The pitch-to-distance rule applies to a source you can see. Replace a 2 mm LED with an 8 mm bounced pool and the rule relaxes hard — 60 LEDs may well be enough, which is much closer to your instinct than the 120 I gave you. It has to be measured rather than asserted, but 60 is now the number to test first, not the fallback.

The cost of firing down is the thermal path. The strip goes back onto printed plastic — a ledge inside the channel — instead of onto the stainless ring, which was one of the better arguments for the move. Two ways out: a thin aluminium ledge instead of a printed one, or fire up with a low white baffle shielding the diffuser, which keeps the strip on steel and still makes the light indirect. Fig 3 draws all three.

How many LEDs, revised

The counts below are the direct case, and they are the pessimistic bound — what you would need if the diffuser could see the chips. They are worth keeping because they tell you where 120 came from, and because the arithmetic on the readout side does not change.

On the readout side nothing is demanding: 36 already gives 10° steps, and dimming the last lit LED slides the arc's end between positions, so a blended band moves continuously rather than stepping. Every count in this table is more than enough to show 40 %. The number is chosen entirely by how the band looks.

60 is also the right number for the object. Six degrees per LED is exactly one per knob detent — the light and the mechanism then share a unit, and a single detent of rotation is a single step of the arc. If the bench says 60 ripples, 120 gives you two per detent and the same logic holds. 90 does not divide into 60 and is the one to avoid.

What thickening buys

  • Roughly nine times the bending stiffness against its current thickest section, and the 0.9 mm foot — the actual failure point — stops existing. Stiffness goes as thickness cubed.
  • Twice the scattering path. Hiding power rises with the distance light travels through the opal, so a 4 mm wall does real optical work that a 1.9 mm one cannot.
  • A part you can machine. A 4 mm web in 6 mm cast opal sheet sits well inside the 1.5 mm CNC minimum wall; the 2 mm web did not.
  • A proper seat. 4 mm of flat bottom face on steel, clamped axially through a compliant gasket, with radial clearance for the 0.09 mm thermal differential and moisture swell.
  • Edges you can chamfer. At 0.9 mm there was nothing to break; at 4 mm every edge gets a 0.3 mm chamfer, which is where cracks otherwise start.

What it costs

  • Transmission. 4 mm of a 36 % grade passes far less than 1.9 mm did. Move up the range — Perspex Opal 030 at ~68 %, or Evonik 0D010 DF at 83 % through 3 mm, which diffuses by beads through the bulk rather than surface texture. That is the right grade for a thick wall anyway.
  • The silhouette changes. A 2 mm proud band between a Ø175.4 knob and a Ø175.4 plate is a visible collar. It should be a decision, not a side effect — but the teaser's strongest frames are the glow, so a band that stands out from the metal may be the better object.
  • The rim ring grows a shelf. 1 mm tall out to r 89.7 on its top face, to carry the overhang. Trivial on a turned part, but it re-opens the ring drawing and the 92 vent openings sit just below it — check the shelf does not close their mouths.
  • The ring takes the knocks now. Standing proud, it is the first thing a sleeve catches. At 4 mm that is survivable; at 0.9 it would not have been.
  • Still no stock tube at Ø179.4. Machined from sheet either way — but 4 mm makes that a routine job rather than a fragile one.

The bench test, revised

One rig now answers three questions. Print a straight channel 100 mm long in black, 4.0 mm wide, with a white floor and a white roof, and a slot at one end for a strip of opal stood up as the outer wall. Make three strips of LEDs at 8.8, 5.9 and 4.4 mm pitch, and mount each so it can face outward, up behind a baffle, or down at the floor. Try 2 mm and 4 mm opal.

Photograph each combination in the dark at fixed exposure from 600 mm. What you are reading off: does 60 LEDs firing down look as good as 120 firing outward? If it does — and the physics says it should — the count halves, the current halves, and the whole thing gets easier.
