# Findings C — Power, noise and established good practice for the 60's audio board

Research strand for the 60's headphone/line output: how well-measured USB-powered DACs (digital-to-analogue converters) get clean analogue power off a dirty 5 V USB rail, what noise floor is plausible with a ring motor ~20 mm away and Wi-Fi on the same board, the checklist of things good desktop designs do, and how to measure the result credibly without an Audio Precision analyzer.

Every figure below is either cited to a fetched source (URL given) or explicitly marked unverified / quote required. Findings and assessment are separated in each section.

---

## 1. Power and regulation practice — what well-measured USB DACs actually do

### 1.1 Findings, with sources

**USB 5 V is treated as raw input, never as a rail.** The consistent pattern across DIY schematic reviews and shipped products is: USB VBUS (the 5 V bus voltage pin) → input filtering (ferrite bead or LC pi filter plus bulk and ceramic capacitance) → a tree of LDOs (low-dropout linear regulators), with separate LDOs for digital logic, clocks, and the analogue DAC rail.

- A diyAudio schematic-review thread for an XMOS + Sabre USB DAC design describes the accepted approach: independent LDOs for the MCU (microcontroller), for clocks/buffer, and for the DAC, with ferrite beads and 10–100 nF capacitors between the USB connector and the 5 V rail ([diyAudio schematic review thread](https://www.diyaudio.com/community/threads/schematic-review-request-usb-dac-amp-design-based-on-xmos-sabre-dac.434351/)). The same thread notes that USB power is not inherently a limiting factor: the Topping D50 III and SMSL SU-1 are cited there as USB-powered designs measuring SINAD (signal to noise-and-distortion ratio) above 110 dB. (That SINAD figure is a forum claim referencing Audio Science Review measurements; not re-verified against the original review pages in this pass — unverified.)
- The same diyAudio discussion cautions that ferrite beads are poorly specified for power-filtering duty — their inductance drops with DC load current in ways manufacturers do not quantify — and prefers proper LC filters for supply cleaning ([diyAudio](https://www.diyaudio.com/community/threads/schematic-review-request-usb-dac-amp-design-based-on-xmos-sabre-dac.434351/)).
- A worked example of USB 5 V input filtering (pi filter: ferrite/inductor with capacitors either side, plus ESD protection) is Andy Brown's write-up ([andybrown.me.uk, "Filtering the 5V USB power supply line"](https://andybrown.me.uk/2015/07/24/usb-filtering/)) and a reference open-hardware USB power filter board using LC filtering plus common-mode choke ([harsh-hw-dev/USB-Power-Filter on GitHub](https://github.com/harsh-hw-dev/USB-Power-Filter)).

**LDO parts.** The two classes named in the task brief are real and current:

- TI TPS7A47: 36 V, 1 A LDO with 4.2 µVRMS output noise and an NR (noise-reduction) pin; TI's own product page positions it for "operational amplifiers, ADCs, DACs, and other high-performance analog circuitry", explicitly for filtering out DC-DC switching ripple in "sensitive instrumentation, audio, and RF applications" ([TI TPS7A47 product page](https://www.ti.com/product/TPS7A47)). This class is oversized for a 5 V-input design (its strength is high input voltage); relevant here mainly as the archetype "big low-noise LDO".
- TI TPS7A20: 300 mA LDO, 1.6–6.0 V input, 7 µVRMS output noise without a noise bypass capacitor, high PSRR (power supply rejection ratio) — sized exactly for post-regulating a 5 V USB-derived rail per-function ([TI TPS7A20 datasheet](https://www.ti.com/lit/ds/symlink/tps7a20.pdf)). (The LP5907 named in the brief is the same class — 250 mA low-noise LDO — usage in specific shipped DACs not verified this pass.)

**Negative rails via charge pump.** TI's LM27762 is a regulated inverting charge pump plus positive and negative low-noise LDOs in one part: ±1.5 V to ±5 V outputs at up to ±250 mA from a 2.7–5.5 V input, 2 MHz switching frequency, with TI naming "DAC biasing" and power amplifiers as target applications and offering independent enable pins for rail sequencing ([TI LM27762 product page](https://www.ti.com/product/LM27762), [datasheet](https://www.ti.com/lit/gpn/LM27762)). The pattern — charge pump inverter followed by a negative LDO to strip the 2 MHz ripple — is the standard way small USB-powered designs get a bipolar analogue supply without an inductor-based converter.

**Or skip the discrete negative rail entirely: the ES9219 integrates it.** The 60's chosen DAC, the ES9219Q (ESS Technology ES9219, Q package variant — see `docs/SOURCING-BOM.md`, which records that the ES9219C is obsolete and the ES9219Q is the buyable part), is a headphone SoC (system on chip) that integrates the analogue plumbing a discrete design would build from the parts above. ESS's launch material states it integrates an "audiophile PLL and LDO, eliminating the need for external regulators and crystal", delivers 2 Vrms output, +130 dB SNR (signal-to-noise ratio) and +121 dB DNR (dynamic range), −114 dB THD+N (total harmonic distortion plus noise) at 1.0 Vrms into 100 kΩ, and integrates "jack sensing with ESS's patented output bypass switching" ([audioXpress on the ES9219](https://audioxpress.com/news/ess-technology-introduces-third-generation-es9219-headphone-soc-for-mobile-devices)). Whether the ES9219's 2 Vrms mode uses an internal charge pump for a ground-centred (capacitor-less) output is stated in the datasheet, which could not be machine-read this pass ([Mouser-hosted ES9219 datasheet PDF](https://www.mouser.com/datasheet/2/1082/ES9219_Datasheet_v1_2-3460042.pdf)) — **unverified; read the datasheet before schematic capture**.

**What a well-regarded desktop maker says about power.** JDS Labs' official Atom DAC release post is the most explicit designer write-up found. Key points, all from [blog.jdslabs.com](https://blog.jdslabs.com/2020/06/jds-labs-atom-dac-official-release-benchmarks/):

- The Atom DAC deliberately uses a dedicated 15 VAC transformer (a $16.99 part in a $99 product) rather than USB power, "ensuring consistency of noise performance" — the transformer "does not radiate power supply switching noise".
- Measured results: SINAD 110 dB, A-weighted noise −109 dBu, A-weighted dynamic range 117 dB, THD+N at 1 kHz < 0.00045 %.
- Clocking: a SiTime MEMS oscillator improved USB jitter components at 12 kHz by 8 dB versus the previous design.
- On sharing one supply between DAC and amp: "This will result in ground loop … likely audible."
- Design sensitivity at this level: "even small changes to firmware like LED intensity can degrade benchmarks" — directly relevant to the 60's halo LEDs.

**Ground topology.** Three sources converge:

- Espressif's ESP32 audio design guidelines (the closest thing to vendor guidance for "Wi-Fi SoC plus codec on one board"): give the Wi-Fi section, the codec, the power amplifier and LEDs their own reference ground planes, joined at a single point through a 0 Ω resistor of at least 0603 package; and "the GND of the Wi-Fi circuit and that of other high-power devices should be separated and connected through wires if there are high-power components, such as motors, in the design" ([Espressif ESP32 Audio Design Guidelines PDF](https://www.espressif.com/sites/default/files/documentation/esp32_audio_design_guidelines__en.pdf), [ESP-ADF project design guide](https://docs.espressif.com/projects/esp-adf/en/latest/design-guide/project-design.html)).
- hifisonix's ground-loops paper (Bonsai): signal ground connects to chassis at a single point near the input connectors; connector shields go to chassis ground, not signal ground; input and output pairs are tightly twisted to minimise loop area ([hifisonix Ground Loops article](https://www.hifisonix.com/articles/ground-loops/), [PDF](https://hifisonix.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/Ground-Loops.pdf)).
- The USB-specific trap, from the same hifisonix material: a PC's USB connector shell is internally bonded to the PC chassis (and thus mains earth), so plugging in USB can short out any ground-lift/separation resistors between audio ground and chassis ground in the peripheral. The peripheral's USB ground **is** the PC's earth; the design must assume that, not fight it.

### 1.2 Assessment (not sourced — engineering judgement on the above)

For the 60: take VBUS through a pi filter, run the ESP32-P4, display and halo LEDs from switching regulators as usual, and give the ~25 × 25 mm audio board its own filtered feed and its own LDO(s) — TPS7A20-class is the right size at these currents. Because the ES9219Q integrates its own LDO and output stage, the external job reduces to: one quiet pre-regulated rail, kept away from motor and LED return currents. The single most important decision is layout/grounding, not regulator selection: one ground plane per Espressif's guidance with a deliberate single tie point, motor drive currents given their own return path that never crosses the audio board's ground reference, and the steel base bonded to USB ground at one point near the USB-C connector (it is already at PC earth potential through the cable — pretending otherwise creates the loop). The JDS Labs LED remark says the halo PWM (pulse-width modulation) dimming frequency and its return routing must be treated as an audio-relevant design input, not cosmetics.

## 2. The motor-proximity question — what noise floor is realistic

### 2.1 Findings, with sources

Direct measurements of "audio output with a haptic motor N mm away" are scarce; the best available evidence is devices that ship exactly that combination and have been measured:

- **Phones.** The LG G7 ThinQ — an ESS ES9218P quad-DAC headphone output inside a phone that also contains a vibration motor, cellular radios and Wi-Fi — measured 105 dB SINAD on Audio Science Review's Audio Precision rig ([ASR review of LG G7 ThinQ](https://www.audiosciencereview.com/forum/index.php?threads/review-and-audio-measurement-of-lg-g7-thinq-smartphone.4468/)). This is the strongest single data point: an ES9218-family part (one generation before the 60's ES9219Q) achieves ~105 dB SINAD in a far more hostile, far smaller enclosure than the 60.
- **Dongles.** The Apple USB-C to 3.5 mm adapter (Cirrus Logic CS46L06 bridge DAC) measures 98 dB SINAD / 113 dB SNR with 0.9 Ω output impedance, hanging directly off a phone's radios ([ASR forum measurements summary](https://www.audiosciencereview.com/forum/index.php?threads/apple-dongle-usb-c-to-3-5-mm-adc-quality-with-earpods-free-headsets.34219/)).
- **Wi-Fi on the same board as the DAC.** The WiiM Pro Plus — a Wi-Fi streamer with the DAC on the same small board as the Wi-Fi SoC — measures about 115 dB SINAD at its full 2.083 Vrms output on ASR's rig ([ASR WiiM Pro Plus review](https://www.audiosciencereview.com/forum/index.php?threads/wiim-pro-plus-review-measurements-streamer.47020/)); an independent test measured 111.8 dB versus 85.4 dB for the original WiiM Pro ([WiiM forum summary of tests](https://forum.wiimhome.com/threads/my-wiim-pro-plus-tests.1235/)). The original WiiM Mini's analogue output was judged "lackluster" by ASR while its digital output was clean ([ASR WiiM Mini review](https://www.audiosciencereview.com/forum/index.php?threads/wiim-mini-review-streamer.33099/)) — evidence that Wi-Fi coexistence is a solved problem when the analogue section is done carefully and a real problem when it is not.
- **Motor drive EMI (electromagnetic interference) practice.** Guidance for PWM motor drivers sharing a board with sensitive analogue: keep the H-bridge switching loop tight; separate the motor power domain from the signal domain, ideally from different supplies; a continuous ground plane between power and signal layers to limit common-mode loop area; do not route the two power rails in parallel for long distances ([ST community thread on PWM noise mitigation](https://community.st.com/t5/stm32-mcus-products/pwm-dithering-for-noise-mitigation/td-p/716189) and linked practice). Espressif's audio guideline quoted in section 1 explicitly names motors as the case where grounds must be separated and joined by wire at one point ([Espressif audio design guidelines](https://www.espressif.com/sites/default/files/documentation/esp32_audio_design_guidelines__en.pdf)).
- **Does the steel base shield?** Steel helps at low frequency but is a poor substitute for high-permeability alloy: properly annealed mu-metal has permeability on the order of 100× ordinary steel ([Hackaday, "What's Mu Metal?"](https://hackaday.com/2026/08/31/whats-mu-metal/)). A diyAudio thread on hum pickup reports (forum claim, not a controlled measurement) that one wrap of 1/8 in (3.2 mm) mu-metal absorbs about 75 % of a magnetic field and two layers about 90 % ([diyAudio magnetic shielding thread](https://www.diyaudio.com/community/threads/magnetic-shielding-how-to-defeat-hum-pickup.405352/page-3)). Mu-metal is ineffective above roughly 100 kHz, where eddy-current (conductive) shielding takes over, and loses its properties when bent or drilled unless re-annealed (same sources).

### 2.2 Assessment

Three coupling paths from the ring motor at ~20 mm, in descending order of danger:

1. **Shared-supply coupling** — motor coil current drawn from the same 5 V budget modulates VBUS. Killed by the LDO tree's PSRR plus input filtering; this is the well-trodden path and the reason section 1 matters more than shielding.
2. **Ground return coupling** — motor return current crossing the audio ground reference. Killed by layout (single tie point, separate return paths), not by any component.
3. **Magnetic near-field coupling** — the motor's field inducing current directly into output traces, jack wiring, or an inductor in the signal path. Mitigated by keeping the output loop area tiny (jack directly on the audio board — which the 60's architecture already does), and only secondarily by shielding. The steel base helps somewhat as a low-frequency flux path and as an eddy-current shield for RF, but the evidence says do not count on the enclosure to rescue a bad layout; if a shield is ever needed it is a small mu-metal can over the audio board, bought annealed, not the structural steel.

One structural advantage the 60 has over every phone measured above: the motor makes noise only while the ring is being turned, and detent events are milliseconds long. Steady-state measured noise floor (the ASR-style dashboard) is taken with the motor idle; the honest engineering target is (a) idle noise floor limited by the DAC, and (b) no audible artefact during rotation. Given the LG G7 ThinQ's 105 dB SINAD with the same DAC family in a phone, a realistic target for the 60 is: **idle SINAD limited by the ES9219Q itself (ESS claims −114 dB THD+N; ASR-measured ES9219 dongles cluster near 100–110 dB SINAD — the exact figure for comparable dongles is unverified this pass)**, with motor-off noise indistinguishable from motor-present-but-idle noise. That last equivalence is a bench-measurable acceptance criterion and should become one.

## 3. Established-practice checklist

Each item: what it is, why, who does it / example implementation, example part with source.

### 3.1 Grounding scheme
- **What:** one ground plane per functional domain (Wi-Fi/digital, audio analogue, motor power, LEDs) joined at a single deliberate point; connector shells and chassis bonded to chassis/USB ground at one point near the entry connector; signal ground never used as a shield ground.
- **Why:** every measured failure mode in sections 1–2 (ground loops, motor return currents, LED PWM) is a ground-routing failure.
- **Who/how:** Espressif's audio design guideline (0 Ω resistor ≥ 0603 as the tie point, motors on separated grounds) ([PDF](https://www.espressif.com/sites/default/files/documentation/esp32_audio_design_guidelines__en.pdf)); hifisonix single-point signal-to-chassis bond near input connectors ([article](https://www.hifisonix.com/articles/ground-loops/)); JDS Labs' warning that sharing one supply across two grounded boxes creates an audible loop ([JDS Labs blog](https://blog.jdslabs.com/2020/06/jds-labs-atom-dac-official-release-benchmarks/)).
- **60-specific:** the USB-C ground is the PC's earth (hifisonix); bond the steel base to it at the connector, once.

### 3.2 Jack insertion detection and switching
- **What:** a 3.5 mm jack with an internal mechanical switch actuated by plug insertion, read by firmware (mute before the plug seats, unmute after; mute on removal).
- **Why:** insertion drags the plug tip across the ring and sleeve contacts — without detection the amp drives into momentary shorts and the user hears scratching at full level.
- **Example part (verified from datasheet):** Same Sky (formerly CUI Devices) **SJ-3524-SMT-TR** — 3.5 mm stereo, right-angle surface mount, 4 pins: sleeve, tip, ring, tip switch (pin 4 contacts the tip terminal with no plug present and opens on insertion — the classic normally-closed detection contact). Body 14.5 mm × 6 mm × 5 mm (length × width × height, from the mechanical drawing), rated 12 VDC / 1 A, 50 mΩ max contact resistance, 5,000 cycle life, −25 to +85 °C. Datasheet: [sameskydevices.com SJ-352X-SMT series PDF](https://www.sameskydevices.com/product/resource/sj-352x-smt.pdf); product page: [SJ-3524-SMT-TR](https://www.sameskydevices.com/product/interconnect/connectors/audio-connectors/jacks/sj-3524-smt-tr); stocked at [Digi-Key](https://www.digikey.com/en/products/detail/cui-devices/SJ-3524-SMT-TR/281298) and [Mouser](https://www.mouser.com/en/ProductDetail/Same-Sky/SJ-3524-SMT-TR-BE). The switchless sibling is the SJ-3523-SMT-TR.
- **Alternatives (located, dimensions not verified this pass):** Kycon STX-3120 series, 3.5 mm PCB-mount stereo jacks with 3- and 5-position variants ([Kycon STX-3120 datasheet PDF](https://www.kycon.com/Catalog_PDF/STX3120.pdf), [Mouser STX-3120-3BM](https://www.mouser.com/ProductDetail/Kycon/STX-3120-3BM?qs=UDNZyWnowiEwFg0zQD1l8Q%3D%3D)). Lumberg 1503-series switching jacks exist but were not fetched — **unverified**.
- **60-specific note:** the ES9219Q integrates jack sensing with output bypass switching per ESS ([audioXpress](https://audioxpress.com/news/ess-technology-introduces-third-generation-es9219-headphone-soc-for-mobile-devices)); a mechanical switch contact on the jack remains worth routing to a GPIO (general-purpose input/output pin) as the belt to that chip-level braces. A rear jack also serves line-out; detection lets firmware distinguish "nothing plugged" for the mute logic and UI.

### 3.3 Pop/click suppression at power-on and connect
- **What:** keep the output at 0 V while rails settle, then ramp; on shutdown, mute before rails collapse.
- **Why:** rail sequencing transients otherwise appear at the jack as a pop — at 2 Vrms scale into sensitive IEMs (in-ear monitors) it is loud and, per Audio Science Review culture, reviewers call it out.
- **Who/how:** Schiit's Bifrost and Gungnir use a muting relay that also clicks on S/PDIF stream interruption ([Head-Fi discussion of Schiit muting behaviour](https://www.head-fi.org/threads/is-clicking-popping-on-dac-normal.726490/page-2)); relay-across-output (shorting relay) and MOSFET (metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistor) mute switches are the two standard implementations, with FET mutes avoiding the relay's own click when properly sequenced ([Gearspace on mute relays and clicks](https://gearspace.com/threads/mute-relays-and-clicks-pops.754918/)); patented three-stage power-on sequencing circuits describe the general shape (hold off, ramp, release) ([US patent 8447046](https://patents.justia.com/patent/8447046)). Mobile-derived DAC SoCs like the ES9219 handle ramping internally as part of their power-state machine — the specific soft-ramp register behaviour of the ES9219Q is in its datasheet, **unverified this pass**.
- **60-specific:** with the ES9219Q's internal switch plus firmware control of power states, a mechanical relay is likely unnecessary; the criterion is bench-verified silence at connect/disconnect/power-loss, not a particular circuit.

### 3.4 Mute on unplug / on USB disconnect
- **What:** firmware mutes the DAC the moment jack-detect opens or VBUS drops.
- **Why:** prevents the half-inserted-plug scratch (section 3.2) and the dying-rail pop (section 3.3); on unplug of USB the device browns out — the mute must beat the brownout.
- **Who/how:** implemented as firmware policy on jack-detect switches and VBUS comparators; universally present in phone audio stacks (the jack-sense pin on every mobile codec exists for this). No single citable teardown found this pass — the mechanism is the SJ-3524's pin-4 switch plus the ES9219's jack sensing above.

### 3.5 Output short-circuit / overcurrent protection
- **What:** the output stage survives an indefinite short to ground (which every plug insertion causes briefly).
- **Why:** warranty and safety; a 60-unit numbered run cannot absorb field failures from something as routine as plugging in headphones.
- **Who/how:** integrated headphone amplifiers of the ES9219's class carry built-in current limiting as standard mobile-qualification practice — **the ES9219Q's specific short-circuit rating is a datasheet item, unverified this pass**. Discrete designs get it from the op-amp's internal current limit or a series resistance. Action: confirm in the ES9219 datasheet before relying on it.

### 3.6 ESD (electrostatic discharge) protection on the jack
- **What:** a low-capacitance TVS (transient voltage suppressor) diode array from tip/ring to ground at the jack.
- **Why:** a 3.5 mm plug carried in a pocket arrives charged; the jack contacts deliver that strike straight into the DAC output pins. IEC 61000-4-2 (the international ESD immunity standard) level 4 is ±8 kV contact discharge.
- **Example parts:** TI **TPD2E001** — dual-channel TVS array, 1.5 pF line capacitance, rated for ±8 kV IEC 61000-4-2 level 4, 1 nA max leakage ([TI product page](https://www.ti.com/product/TPD2E001)); Nexperia **PESD5V0S1UL** — single-line 5 V ESD clamp, 0.7 pF, SC-79 package, positioned for audio-line protection ([icnavigator overview of TVS for audio ports](https://icnavigator.com/technology/efuse-hot-swap-or-ing-protection/tvs-diodes-for-audio-port-protection/)); Littelfuse's SP-series TVS arrays and design guide cover the same ground ([Littelfuse ESD protection design guide PDF](https://www.farnell.com/datasheets/1750905.pdf)).
- **Caveat (assessment):** TVS parts on an audio line must be chosen for voltage headroom above the 2 Vrms (≈ ±2.83 V peak) swing and checked for capacitance/nonlinearity at signal level — a clamp that begins conducting near the signal peaks will show up in THD. This is a bench check, not a datasheet check.

### 3.7 DC blocking: capacitor-coupled vs DC-coupled
- **What:** either a series output capacitor blocks DC from reaching the headphones, or the output is DC-coupled and offset is managed actively (DC servo, ground-centred output) with protection for the failure case.
- **Why/trade-offs (from diyAudio practice):** the output capacitor's sole functional advantage is protecting the load if the amp fails with DC on its output; large electrolytics in the signal path add distortion and low-frequency roll-off into low-impedance loads. DC-coupled designs need either a servo (corrects millivolt-scale offset) and/or a relay with DC-sense (disconnects on fault) ([diyAudio "Output caps or no output caps"](https://www.diyaudio.com/community/threads/output-caps-or-no-output-caps.196517/), [diyAudio capacitor-coupled output thread](https://www.diyaudio.com/community/threads/capacitor-coupled-output.373515/)).
- **What the well-measured products chose:** modern well-measured designs are DC-coupled at the jack. Mobile DAC SoCs achieve this with an internal negative rail so the output is ground-centred with no blocking capacitor (the Apple dongle and dongle-class devices measured on ASR are capacitor-less at the jack — inferred from sub-1 Ω output impedances and mobile codec architecture; the specific ES9219Q output topology is a datasheet item, **unverified this pass**). Desktop discrete designs (JDS Labs, Schiit) are DC-coupled with relay/mute protection rather than output capacitors (Schiit muting relay citation in 3.3; JDS Atom output topology **unverified** beyond its measured performance).
- **60-specific (assessment):** if the ES9219Q's ground-centred output is confirmed, DC blocking hardware disappears from the BOM (bill of materials) and the checklist item becomes "verify no more than a few mV DC at the jack on the bench across power states".

### 3.8 Clock placement
- **What:** the audio master clock lives next to the DAC, not next to the Wi-Fi SoC; clock supply is decoupled from digital rails via ferrite/LDO.
- **Who/how:** diyAudio review practice: ferrite-bead decoupling of clock circuits from the rest of the digital domain ([diyAudio schematic review](https://www.diyaudio.com/community/threads/schematic-review-request-usb-dac-amp-design-based-on-xmos-sabre-dac.434351/)); JDS Labs' measured 8 dB improvement in 12 kHz jitter components from a SiTime MEMS oscillator choice ([JDS Labs blog](https://blog.jdslabs.com/2020/06/jds-labs-atom-dac-official-release-benchmarks/)); the ES9219 integrates its PLL (phase-locked loop) and needs no external crystal per ESS ([audioXpress](https://audioxpress.com/news/ess-technology-introduces-third-generation-es9219-headphone-soc-for-mobile-devices)), and its Time Domain Jitter Eliminator is ESS's asynchronous-clock defence ([Mouser ES9219 datasheet listing](https://www.mouser.com/datasheet/2/1082/ES9219_Datasheet_v1_0_4-1923574.pdf)).

- **Software:** **REW (Room EQ Wizard)** — free; frequency response, stepped-sine distortion at up to 96 points per octave, real-time analyser ([roomeqwizard.com](https://www.roomeqwizard.com/)); **Multitone Analyzer** by Paul Kane (pkane) — free; loopback measurement of THD+N, IMD (intermodulation distortion), frequency response, clock drift ([distortaudio.org/multitone](https://distortaudio.org/multitone.html)); ASR members publish amplifier test series with it ([ASR thread](https://www.audiosciencereview.com/forum/index.php?threads/amplifier-sample-test-reviews-using-pauls-multitone-analyzer-software.41681/)). ARTA is the third common package (shareware; price **quote required**). Neurochrome documents the general soundcard-rig method ([Measuring Distortion on the Cheap](https://neurochrome.com/pages/measuring-distortion-on-the-cheap)).
- **Loads:** headphone testing per ASR convention uses 32 Ω and 300 Ω resistive loads. At the 60's 2 Vrms these dissipate 125 mW and 13 mW respectively (calculated, not sourced), so ordinary 5 W power resistors suffice; exact resistor prices not fetched — low cost, **quote required**.

### 4.2 Assessment — minimal credible rig for the 60

A Cosmos ADCiso Grade A (~$260 street) + REW + Multitone + a small board of switched 32 Ω / 300 Ω power resistors measures the same core metrics ASR publishes (SINAD/THD+N at 1 kHz, noise floor, dynamic range, frequency response, multitone, output impedance via loaded/unloaded delta) with a converter whose own floor — 128–129 dB(A) SNR at Grade A — sits below anything the 60 will produce, and whose USB isolation removes the rig's own ground loop. Total under **$300 plus resistors**. The QA403 at $599 adds a calibrated generator and a one-box workflow; the better choice if the bench must also test third-party gear or needs absolute-level calibration with less care. The APx555 remains the instrument reviewers use — the 60 only has to survive it, not own it. Credibility practice: publish the rig, levels and load for every figure; the E1DA-plus-REW combination is already normalised on ASR, so numbers from it are taken seriously when the method is stated.
