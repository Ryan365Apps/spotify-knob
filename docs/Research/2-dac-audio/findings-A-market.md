# Findings A — Market and competitor survey: desk audio, DAC/amps, and knob-DAC hybrids

Research date: 2026-09-02. Scope: what buyers of a KEF-LSX-class desk audio setup use and pay today; survey of desktop DAC (digital-to-analogue converter) / headphone-amplifier products and knob-led controllers; and where the 60 — the Ø135 mm premium dial with an onboard DAC and 3.5 mm output — sits in that landscape.

All prices and measurements below are cited to a fetched source or marked **unverified**. SINAD = signal-to-noise-and-distortion ratio, in dB, as popularised by Audio Science Review (audiosciencereview.com — restated at each use as "Audio Science Review"). THD+N = total harmonic distortion plus noise. SNR = signal-to-noise ratio. Where a number came from a search-result summary of a page rather than a direct fetch of that page, it is marked "(via search snippet)" and should be treated as one notch less reliable.

---

## 1. What this buyer uses for desk audio today, and what they pay (findings)

- **Powered desk speakers, KEF class.** KEF LSX II: US $1,500/pair list; the cut-down LSX II LT: US $1,000/pair list, and both are frequently discounted hard (LT seen at US $500–700 in 2026). Source: Gear Patrol sale coverage, https://www.gearpatrol.com/audio/kef-lsx-ii-lt-active-speaker-sale/ (via search snippet). KEF positions the LSX II LT explicitly as a desktop system.
- **A DAC/headphone-amp box or stack next to the monitor.** The market is sharply tiered:
  - US $9 (Apple USB-C dongle) — measurably transparent for sensitive headphones.
  - US $100–300 — the volume zone: FiiO K11 (~US $129), Schiit Magni Unity with DAC card (US $199), Topping DX3 Pro+ (US $199), FiiO K7 (~US $200), Topping DX5 II (US $299). Almost all of these measure at or beyond audible transparency.
  - US $500–800 — the "nice object" zone: JDS Labs Element IV (US $549), Chord Mojo 2 (US $650–799 depending on market/date).
  - US $1,100–1,300 — the desk endgame: RME ADI-2 DAC FS, US $1,299 list (via search snippet, equipboard.com/items/rme-adi-2-dac-fs). This is the reference "knob-and-screen desk DAC" many audiophiles graduate to.
- **Implication (finding, not opinion):** the price the 60 will ask sits *above* almost every desktop DAC/amp this buyer owns, and at or above the price of their speakers' electronics. The DAC inside the 60 will be compared, by this exact buyer, against boxes from US $129 up that measure transparently.

**Key structural finding:** in this whole survey, no shipping product combines a *premium physical dial* with an *onboard measurement-grade DAC and headphone output* in one desk object. The desk-controller category (Stream Deck +, BEACN, Loupedeck) is plastic, streamer-aimed, and DAC-less or spec-silent; the DAC/amp category has knobs as controls, not as the object. The closest philosophical neighbours are the JDS Labs Element IV (a DAC/amp *shaped around* its knob) and the RME ADI-2 DAC FS (the desk-endgame box audiophiles actually keep). The 60 would be first in its exact niche.

## 3. Reviewer sentiment (findings)

**What gets praised in this category:**

- **Measured transparency at low prices.** The Audio Science Review audience treats ~115 dB+ SINAD (signal-to-noise-and-distortion ratio) at US $113–299 (Topping DX1 II, DX3 Pro+, DX5 II) as the norm. Praise language: "top tier", "exceptional", golfing-panther poll ratings.
- **Quiet noise floors for sensitive in-ear monitors.** The Topping L30 review singles out its −9 dB gain mode; the DX5 II is praised for a 1.8 µVrms noise floor (manufacturer figure). Reviewers explicitly test 50 mV output performance.
- **Low output impedance.** The Apple dongle at 0.9 Ω and the Element IV at 0.22 Ω are called out approvingly; the Audioengine D1 at 10 Ω would be criticised today.
- **The knob itself, when done well.** The JDS Labs Element IV marketing and customer reception centre on the knob: a discrete optical encoder, "large steel shaft for buttery smooth rotation without wobble", 0.5 dB steps — and the industrial design is literally a flat box that exists to present one large top-mounted dial. headphones.com calls the package "arguably the best option for a DAC/Amp combo under $1000". This is the strongest evidence in the survey that a knob-led object resonates with exactly the buyer of the 60 — and that buyers will pay ~US $550 for a knob-led box whose electronics are merely (very) good.
- **On-device EQ (equalisation) as a differentiator.** Both the Element IV (12-band parametric, web app) and DX5 II (10-band) reviews treat built-in parametric EQ as a headline feature.

**What gets criticised:**

- **A weak DAC section beside a strong amp** — the defining flaw pattern. FiiO K5 Pro (line out poor at 2 V), Schiit Magni Unity ("reluctantly recommending… Company can and should do better"), original iFi Zen DAC (~100 dB SINAD, "won't hold a candle"). The lesson: reviewers forgive modest power; they do not forgive a DAC that measures below its price peers.
- **Price without measurements to match.** The Chord Mojo 2 is recommended but with the caveat "the price is up there"; its ~90 mW into 32 Ω (approximate, from graph) is an order of magnitude below same-price desktop rivals.
- **Software/interaction papercuts** — the Element IV EQ-disable behaviour ("you are unable to simply turn it back on"); the BEACN Mix Create steep, poorly documented software.
- **The iFi subjectivist positioning** splits reviewers: loved by What Hi-Fi/TechRadar (Zen DAC 3, £229), unmeasured or unimpressive on the measurement sites.

## 4. Positioning conclusion (opinion / recommendation — separated from findings)

**Where the 60 sits.** There is a genuine hole: nobody sells a premium machined *dial* that is also a legitimate audio endpoint. But the hole is narrow and its walls are hard. On one side, US $129–299 boxes (FiiO K11, Topping DX3 Pro+/DX5 II) are audibly transparent and heavily measured; on the other, the JDS Element IV at US $549 already owns "the knob-led DAC/amp done right", and the RME ADI-2 DAC FS at US $1,299 owns the desk endgame. The DAC in the 60 therefore cannot be sold on sound quality — it is sold as *completeness*: the object on your desk is also, honestly and measurably, a clean output. The moment it measures worse than the line out of a US $129 FiiO K11, the reviews this buyer reads will say so, and the "everything judged on measured quality" promise collapses.

**The bar it must clear (targets derived from the cited landscape, not measurements):**

- **SINAD (signal-to-noise-and-distortion ratio): ≥ 105 dB at 2 Vrms at the 3.5 mm output.** That clears the Apple dongle (~98 dB), the original Zen DAC (~100 dB), and the FiiO K11 balanced headphone out (~97 dB); parity with the K11 line out (108.6 dB) is the honest minimum, ≥ 110 dB (Topping E30 territory) the safe target. Chasing 120 dB is unnecessary — the Element IV proves ~120 dB is available at US $549; the 60 will not win that race and should not enter it.
- **Noise floor: ≤ 5 µVrms A-weighted** at minimum gain. This buyer plugs in sensitive in-ear monitors to test; the DX5 II at 1.8 µVrms and FiiO K7 at <4.4 µV set expectations. Audible hiss with in-ear monitors is a review-killing flaw regardless of SINAD.
- **Output impedance: ≤ 1 Ω.** Everything credible in the table is ≤ 1 Ω (Apple 0.9 Ω, Element IV 0.22 Ω, Schiit <0.1 Ω, FiiO <1–2.4 Ω). The Audioengine 10 Ω is the cautionary tale.
- **Output power: ≥ 150–300 mW into 32 Ω and ≥ 30–60 mW into 300 Ω** (≈ 2–4 Vrms capability into 300 Ω). That is "drives real headphones cleanly" — Mojo 2-class, not Magni-class — and defensible for a 3.5 mm-on-the-base secondary output, since the primary story of the 60 is control and the owner of a 300 Ω flagship headphone already owns an amp. What is not defensible is dongle-class droop into low-impedance loads at moderate volume.
- **The ES9219Q — the DAC chip chosen in `docs/SOURCING-BOM.md`, the sourcing document for the 60 — plays in this band**: it is a mobile-class ESS part whose realistic implemented performance (roughly the 100–110 dB SINAD class at ~2 Vrms) lands exactly across the "honest minimum to safe target" range above. This sentence is an assessment; the implemented numbers must come from the bench, not the datasheet.
- **Publish the measurements.** Given the doctrine that anything embarrassing under measurement is out, the strongest positioning move this survey suggests: ship a per-unit measurement sheet with each numbered unit. Nobody in the knob/controller category can do that; everybody in the DAC category expects it.

**Pricing signal.** The Element IV at US $549 is the single most informative comparable: knob-led, US-built, measurement-transparent, and called the sub-US $1,000 benchmark by the press. The 60 asking a multiple of that is only coherent because it competes on machining, mass, detents and control — the DAC just has to never be the sentence a reviewer writes second.
