# Section B — Proportion and stance

Research strand for the 60 by Cadrane (the premium desk dial: a Ø135 mm round touchscreen puck with a heavy knurled metal knob around the screen, on a steel base, 34 mm tall). The question: what makes an object on a desk read as permanent rather than portable, and what will the 60's settled proportions — height-to-diameter ratio of roughly 0.25 (34 mm tall on Ø135 mm) and a 2.8 mm shadow line under the knob — read as. Diameter, height and the knob-dominant proportion are settled; this document is about executing the stance well, not reopening those decisions.

Method note on ratios: for round objects the ratio given is height ÷ diameter; for rectangular objects it is height ÷ width as seen from the front. Every dimension below is cited or marked. Ratios computed from cited dimensions are arithmetic, not new claims.

## Findings, object by object

### Apple HomePod mini

- Dimensions: 84.3 mm high, 97.9 mm wide, 345 g — [Apple tech specs](https://www.apple.com/homepod-mini/specs/). Height-to-diameter ratio ≈ 0.86.
- Meets the desk: a flat circular base; the mini's base is plastic where the full-size HomePod used vibration-damping silicone (the silicone version famously marked oiled wood — [AppleInsider on the silicone bottom](https://appleinsider.com/articles/18/02/14/homepods-silicone-bottom-is-causing-rings-on-some-finished-wood-surfaces), [MacRumors on the mini's changed base](https://www.macrumors.com/2020/11/12/homepod-mini-wont-leave-white-rings/)). The sphere is tangent to the desk plane; there is no plinth and almost no visible foot, so the object reads as *placed*, not *installed*.
- Mass signal: none, really. 345 g of mesh-wrapped sphere. Permanence comes entirely from the captive power cord. It is the counter-example: a near-spherical ratio with no base articulation reads as an ornament you could pocket.

### Nest Learning Thermostat (the original Tony Fadell design; figures below are the 2nd and 3rd generation, which kept the original's form)

- Dimensions: 2nd generation, 83.0 mm diameter × 32.0 mm deep, 252 g, stainless steel ring; 3rd generation, 84 mm diameter × 30.8 mm assembled height, 243.7 g — [Google Nest official spec comparison](https://support.google.com/googlenest/answer/9230098?hl=en). That page does not list the 2011 1st generation; its diameter is reported as 3.27 in (83 mm) on [Wikipedia's Nest Thermostat entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nest_Thermostat) (secondary source). Height-to-diameter ratio ≈ 0.37–0.39.
- Meets the surface: it is wall-mounted, so "the desk" is the wall — the body sits proud on a slightly smaller-diameter mounting plate, giving a shadow reveal all round so the steel ring appears to hover a few millimetres off the wall (no published reveal dimension found; the hover effect is visible in any product photo — [Google Store product page with imagery](https://store.google.com/gb/product/nest_learning_thermostat_3rd_gen_specs?hl=en-GB)).
- Mass signal: a full-depth machined stainless ring at ~250 g in an 83 mm package — a quarter kilogram in a palm-sized disc. The mass is real and the ring's mirror finish reflects the wall, which visually detaches it from its background. This is the closest formal ancestor of the 60: a rotating metal ring around a round screen, ratio ~0.38, and it reads as an *instrument*, not an appliance.

### Jabra Speak2 75 — the warning case

- Dimensions: 35 mm high, 132.5 mm wide/deep, 466 g — [Jabra's official tech sheet (PDF)](https://www.jabra.com/_/media/Jabra_VXi_Product-Documentation/Jabra-Speak2-75/Technical-specifications/RevD/EN-Speak2-75-Tech-Spechs-150125.pdf), corroborated by [IP Phone Warehouse](https://www.ipphone-warehouse.com/jabra-speak2-75-uc-usb-bluetooth-speakerphone/). Height-to-width ratio ≈ 0.26.
- Meets the desk: flat-bottomed plastic puck with a fabric top; ships with a travel pouch. It is explicitly designed to be picked up and taken to meetings.
- Mass signal: deliberately low — 466 g of plastic and fabric.
- Why it matters: **the Speak2 75's envelope (Ø132.5 × 35 mm, ratio 0.26) is almost exactly the 60's (Ø135 × 34 mm, ratio 0.25).** The conference-speakerphone puck is the strongest existing association for this exact silhouette on a desk. The proportion itself will not signal permanence; only mass, material and the base treatment can pull the 60 out of that category.

### Dieter Rams — Braun T3 pocket radio, Braun SK4 record player, Braun ET66 calculator

- Braun T3 pocket radio (1958, Rams with the Ulm school): 8.3 × 15.2 × 4.1 cm — [MoMA collection entry with image](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/4134). A hand object; its perforated speaker circle and thumbwheel are graphic ancestors of every round interface since, but its proportions (a flat card) read portable by design. Useful here only as a control: flatness plus light weight plus no base = portable.
- Braun SK4 record player ("Snow White's Coffin", 1956, Rams and Hans Gugelot): 24.1 × 58.4 × 29.2 cm — [MoMA collection entry with image](https://www.moma.org/collection/works/2649), [V&A entry](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O168285). Height-to-width ratio ≈ 0.41. It meets the surface on a full-width white metal chassis with wooden end cheeks and a minimal reveal; the controls sit on the top plane so the object's stance is a low, wide slab you operate from above — the same operating posture as the 60. Permanence is signalled by the enamelled-steel housing and by symmetry: nothing about it suggests a carrying orientation.
- Braun ET66 calculator (Rams and Dietrich Lubs, 1987): approximately 138 × 80 × 15 mm — [Manufactum product listing](https://www.manufactum.com/calculator-braun-a67529/), [V&A entry](https://collections.vam.ac.uk/item/O1360553/et66-calculator-et66-calculator-dieter-rams/) (sources vary by ±2 mm). Portable, but the lesson is the convex circular buttons rising from a matte plane: tactile invitation is created by making the touchable element a different geometry from its ground. The 60's knurled knob against a smooth steel base follows the same rule at a larger scale.

### Bang & Olufsen — Beosound Balance and Beoplay A9

- Beosound Balance: approximately 200 mm diameter × 380 mm high, from 7.2 kg — [B&H product listing](https://www.bhphotovideo.com/c/product/1605828-REG/bang_olufsen_24598vrp_beosound_balance_wireless_multiroom.html), [Luxussound spec page](https://luxussound.com/products/beosound-balance). Height-to-diameter ratio ≈ 1.9. It meets the desk on a solid oak cylinder — a genuine plinth in a heavier-looking material than the knitted body above it. Mass is signalled by putting the visually densest material (wood) at the bottom and the lightest (fabric) at the top. The top face is the interface (touch ring, backlit through anodised aluminium). Lesson: B&O buys permanence with a plinth of honest material, and the interface glows *through* metal rather than from a visible slot.
- Beoplay A9: approximately 908 × 701 × 415 mm on wooden tripod legs, 14.7 kg — [official spec sheet (PDF)](https://content.abt.com/documents/68044/Specification_A9.pdf), [Beoworld entry](https://beoworld.org/beoplay-a9/). The opposite strategy: a huge disc held *off* the floor on thin legs, deliberately floating. Volume is a touch strip on the aluminium rim, not a knob (several retail listings describe it as a dial, but it is capacitive — [Trusted Reviews](https://www.trustedreviews.com/reviews/b-o-a9)). Note for the 60: floating an object on a shadow gap and floating it on legs are the same gesture at different scales — both trade groundedness for elegance.

### Devialet Phantom I

- Dimensions: 255 mm high × 252 mm wide × 342 mm deep, 11.4 kg — [Devialet product page](https://www.devialet.com/en-us/phantom-speaker/phantom-i/phantom-i-108db-gold-stereo/), [What Hi-Fi review](https://www.whathifi.com/reviews/devialet-phantom-i-108db). Height-to-width ratio ≈ 1.0.
- Meets the desk: on a small integral foot under an ovoid body — the body visibly *overhangs* its contact patch on every side.
- Mass signal: extreme density. 11.4 kg in a rugby-ball volume; the polished side domes and chrome-finish details tell you it is dense before you touch it. The Phantom proves an overhung, nearly-floating stance can still read as permanent **if** the density signal is overwhelming. The 60 should note the mechanism: overhang is forgiven when the material honesty (metal, mass) is unmistakable.

### KEF LS50 (Meta) and LSX II

- LS50 Meta: 302 × 200 × 280.5 mm, 7.8 kg each — [KEF product info sheet (PDF)](https://assets.kef.com/documents/ls50/20-KEF-LS50-Meta-Product-Info-Sheet.pdf), [Stereophile specifications](https://www.stereophile.com/content/kef-ls50-meta-loudspeaker-specifications). Height-to-width ratio ≈ 1.5. Meets the shelf flat on its cabinet base (dedicated stands optional); the curved front baffle concentrates visual mass around the single round driver — one circle dominating a monochrome face, the same hierarchy the 60 uses.
- LSX II: approximately 240 × 155 × 180 mm, 3.6 kg each — [KEF USA product page](https://us.kef.com/products/lsx-2/1000), [Sound & Vision review specs](https://www.soundandvision.com/content/kef-lsx-ii-wireless-speaker-system-review-specs). Same composition smaller; noticeably less "permanent" in the room than the LS50, which supports the general point that below some absolute mass the same proportions stop reading as furniture.

### Naim Mu-so (2nd generation) — the best light-under-mass precedent

- Dimensions: 628 mm wide × 122 mm high × 264 mm deep — [Stereophile specifications](https://www.stereophile.com/content/naim-mu-so-2nd-generation-music-system-specifications), [Naim product page](https://www.naimaudio.com/products/mu-so-2nd-generation). Height-to-width ratio ≈ 0.19 — lower than the 60's 0.25, yet nothing about it reads portable.
- Meets the desk: the aluminium chassis sits on a transparent acrylic plinth so the cabinet appears to hover on a lit blade; the mass above is emphasised by the glow below. This is the closest shipping precedent for the 60's downward-firing halo: light at the desk plane, under visible mass, works.
- Mass signal: extruded/anodised aluminium chassis and a large bead-blasted solid-aluminium volume ring, described by Naim as inspired by their Statement amplifier — [Sound & Vision review](https://www.soundandvision.com/content/naim-audio-mu-so-2-wireless-music-system-review), [The Audiophile Man review](https://theaudiophileman.com/mu-so-2nd-generation-review-naim/). No published diameter for the dial itself was found — unverified; reviews consistently single out its weighted spin as the defining touchpoint of the product.

### Marshall Stanmore III and Ruark R1

- Marshall Stanmore III: 412 × 247 × 276 mm, 5.48 kg, with black-and-brass rotary knobs for volume, bass and treble and a brass toggle switch on the top plate — [B&H listing](https://www.bhphotovideo.com/c/product/1734428-REG/marshall_1006014_stanmore_iii_bluetooth_speaker.html), [Marshall product page](https://www.marshall.com/us/en/product/stanmore-iii?pid=1009642). Knobs on the *top* surface, operated from above at a desk or shelf — the knurled brass against black vinyl is a mass-and-material signal borrowed from amplifiers, i.e. from tools.
- Ruark Audio R1 (mk3): 170 × 130 × 135 mm with the "RotoDial" rotary controller on the top face — [Audioholics overview](https://forums.audioholics.com/forums/threads/ruark-audio-r1-mk3-deluxe-tabletop-radio-overview.94208/), [What Hi-Fi review](https://www.whathifi.com/ruark-audio/r1-mk3/review). Same lesson: a top-mounted dial declares "this object lives here and you reach over to it".

### Leica M-series top plate

- Leica M11 body: 139 × 80 × 38.5 mm; the silver version has a brass top plate and weighs 640 g with battery, the black version an aluminium top plate at 530 g — [Photography Blog review](https://www.photographyblog.com/reviews/leica_m11_review), [Leica Store Miami listing](https://leicastoremiami.com/products/leica-m11-black-finish). 110 g of difference from one plate.
- The relevant mechanism: the mass is invisible. Both cameras look identical in photographs; the brass one *feels* different in the hand, and Leica charges for that. Machining marks are absent — the finish is uniform — so mass is signalled by density on pickup and by the knurled, hard-edged control dials standing proud of the plate. For the 60, which "invites idle handling", this is the precedent: the first pickup is a specification, not an accident. The 60's steel base should be specified for felt density the way Leica specifies brass.

### Apple Watch Ultra crown guard

- Case: 49 × 44 × 14.4 mm, titanium — [GSMArena spec listing](https://www.gsmarena.com/apple_watch_ultra-11827.php), [Dimensions.com drawings (good dimensioned images)](https://www.dimensions.com/element/apple-watch-ultra-2022), [Apple's own tech spec page](https://support.apple.com/en-us/111852). (Apple's page and aggregators disagree on how depth is measured — 12 mm vs 14.4 mm appears in different summaries; the 14.4 mm figure includes the sensor dome. Treat exact depth as measurement-convention-dependent.)
- The crown guard is a raised titanium boss that surrounds the digital crown, making the *control* the most three-dimensional feature of the object. It signals seriousness by armouring the input. Translation to the 60: the knob being three-quarters of the visible side is the same statement — the control is the object — and the guard shows that protecting/framing a control with more metal, not less, is what makes it read as an instrument rather than a gadget.

## Cross-cutting observations

1. **Ratio alone does not carry permanence.** The Speak2 75 (0.26, portable by design) and the Nest (0.38, an installed instrument) bracket the 60's 0.25. The Mu-so at 0.19 reads permanent; the HomePod mini at 0.86 reads placeable. What separates them is mass-per-footprint, material honesty at the bottom edge, and whether the interface faces up (you come to it) or out (it could face you anywhere).
2. **Permanent objects put their densest-reading material at the desk plane** (Beosound Balance's oak plinth, Marshall's slab, Mu-so's aluminium chassis over a lit blade). Floating gestures (Beoplay A9 legs, Phantom's overhang) only survive when the density signal is overwhelming.
3. **A lit or shadowed reveal at the base is a proven move** — Mu-so's acrylic glow plinth is the direct precedent — but in every successful case the light is *below* the mass, at desk level, and the seam between mass and mass (knob to base) stays dark. Light in a structural seam reads as a gap; light under the whole object reads as presence.
4. **Top-facing controls are the desk-permanence posture.** Marshall, Ruark, SK4, Mu-so: you reach over and down. The 60's side-gripped knob plus top screen is consistent with this family.

## Recommendations for the 60

1. **Keep the 2.8 mm shadow line genuinely dark, and keep the halo emission below it.** The precedent that works (Naim Mu-so 2nd generation, 628 × 122 mm, hovering on a lit acrylic plinth — [Stereophile](https://www.stereophile.com/content/naim-mu-so-2nd-generation-music-system-specifications)) puts light under the mass at desk level and keeps the metal-to-metal seams dark. If the downward-firing halo leaks into the 2.8 mm gap under the knob, the knob will read as floating on light — contradicting the heavy-instrument story — and the gap will double as a visible dust shelf. Specify the shadow line's internal surfaces in the darkest available finish (black anodise or black oxide, spec to be chosen — unverified) and put a physical baffle between the halo channel and the gap. A 2.8 mm reveal on a Ø135 body is ~2% of diameter, similar in spirit to the Nest's hover reveal off the wall; it is a good width *provided it stays shadow*.
2. **Set a minimum felt mass, and treat it as a requirement, not an outcome.** The 60's envelope is within 3 mm of the Jabra Speak2 75 (Ø132.5 × 35 mm, 466 g, plastic — [Jabra tech sheet](https://www.jabra.com/_/media/Jabra_VXi_Product-Documentation/Jabra-Speak2-75/Technical-specifications/RevD/EN-Speak2-75-Tech-Spechs-150125.pdf)), the one desk object everyone already owns in this silhouette and knows to be portable. The escape route is density on first pickup, exactly as the Leica M11's brass top plate (640 g silver vs 530 g aluminium black — [Photography Blog](https://www.photographyblog.com/reviews/leica_m11_review)) is a paid-for density choice. The Nest packs 252 g into Ø83 × 32 mm ([Google spec page](https://support.google.com/googlenest/answer/9230098?hl=en)); scaled to the 60's footprint that density lands well above 600 g, and the "expensive watch for the desk" positioning argues for a figure comfortably beyond the Speak2 75's 466 g — a target north of 1 kg is my engineering judgment, explicitly **estimated, not sourced**. Whatever the number, write it into the mechanical brief as a floor, and let the steel base plate thickness be sized by it, not by structure alone.
3. **Make the base meet the desk with a concealed foot inset from a crisp edge.** The permanent-reading objects either sit dead flat (Marshall, SK4) or on an articulated plinth (Beosound Balance's oak — [B&H](https://www.bhphotovideo.com/c/product/1605828-REG/bang_olufsen_24598vrp_beosound_balance_wireless_multiroom.html)); none show a visible rubber bumper at the rim. Recess the anti-slip pad (a silicone ring or pads, per the sourcing document `docs/SOURCING-BOM.md`) behind the steel base's perimeter so the visible bottom edge is a continuous dark knife-line at desk level — and note the full-size HomePod's silicone-on-wood staining problem ([AppleInsider](https://appleinsider.com/articles/18/02/14/homepods-silicone-bottom-is-causing-rings-on-some-finished-wood-surfaces)) when choosing pad material against oiled desks. An inset of 6–10 mm from the rim (estimated — verify against the halo optics) keeps the pad invisible at seated eye height while the outward taper of the base still shades it.

## Possibly wrong

1. **The outward taper of the base is fighting the stance, mildly.** The product context says the base "tapers slightly outward as it rises so the light ring looks down at the desk". Every strongly grounded object surveyed gets *wider or stays straight* toward the ground plane (Beosound Balance's oak plinth, Marshall's slab, the SK4 chassis); reverse-taper/overhang appears in the deliberately floating designs (Beoplay A9 on legs, Devialet Phantom's overhung ovoid — which gets away with it only via 11.4 kg of visible density, [Devialet](https://www.devialet.com/en-us/phantom-speaker/phantom-i/phantom-i-108db-gold-stereo/)). The optical goal (halo aimed down) is right; but the taper angle should be the minimum the optics need, and the bottom edge should still present a crisp, wide contact line. If the taper grows past a few degrees the 60 will read as hovering, and combined with a glowing underside that impression compounds. No taper angle is given in the context, so this may already be fine — flagging the direction of the risk, not a measured fault.
2. **The 0.25 ratio's nearest neighbour is a portable conference puck, and the context doesn't acknowledge it.** Ø135 × 34 mm against the Jabra Speak2 75's Ø132.5 × 35 mm is a near-identical envelope ([Jabra tech sheet](https://www.jabra.com/_/media/Jabra_VXi_Product-Documentation/Jabra-Speak2-75/Technical-specifications/RevD/EN-Speak2-75-Tech-Spechs-150125.pdf)). The decision is settled and defensible — the Nest proves a metal ring around a screen at a squat ratio reads as an instrument — but the burden this places on mass and base execution should be stated somewhere in the design documents as a constraint, because it is the single biggest category-confusion risk on a real desk that probably already has a speakerphone on it.
3. Nothing else in the product context looks mistaken against the evidence gathered. The 2.8 mm shadow line specifically is well inside proven proportions (about 2% of body diameter, comparable to the Nest's wall reveal) — the risk is light leakage into it, not its width.
