# Section A — The knurl and the turned edge

Research strand for the 60, the Ø135 mm desk dial by Cadrane. The subject is the knurled outer ring (the knob): what makes knurling on handled objects feel good, what the standards say, and what numbers to cut on a Ø134 mm aluminium ring turned with fingertips. The settled decisions (diamond knurl on the side, coin-edged flat rim, diamond-cut 45° top chamfer after anodising, small inner chamfer, 1.2 mm lower chamfer) are taken as given; this document is about executing them well.

Written 2026-09-02. Every number is either cited to a source URL or explicitly marked estimated/derived/unverified.

---

## 1. The standards: what a knurl actually is

**DIN 82, the German standard for knurl profiles**, is the shared language. It defines the pattern families by letter code, per the Zeus/Dieterle knurling-tool literature and the APPORO summary of the standard:

- **RAA** — straight knurl, grooves parallel to the axis (the "coin edge" pattern).
- **RBL / RBR** — single-direction helical knurl, left- or right-hand, at a 30° or 45° helix angle.
- **RGE** — crossed left+right helix with **raised** diamond points (the classic male diamond knurl).
- **RGV** — crossed helix with **indented** points (female diamond).
- **RKE / RKV** — cross-hatch at 90° (axial + circumferential), points raised or indented.

Sources: [APPORO overview of DIN 82 knurling types](https://apporo-cnc.com/extend.php?menu_s=406&sn=57), [Dieterle/Zeus knurling technology catalogue (PDF)](https://www.dieterle-tools.de/files/dieterle/source/Dieterle/Kataloge/Standard-Aussen/Zeus_Technik_EN.pdf), [Zeus knurling wheels catalogue (PDF)](https://www.defruytier.be/uploads/product/files/zeusknurlingwheelsknurlingtools.pdf). India's mirror standard is [IS 3403:1981, "Dimensions for knurls" (PDF)](https://law.resource.org/pub/in/bis/S01/is.3403.1981.pdf).

Key parameters, per the same sources and the general machining guides:

- **Pitch** — distance between tooth crests. DIN 82 designations embed it: "RAA06 DIN 82" is a straight knurl at 0.6 mm pitch; RGE diamond knurls are commonly supplied at 0.8 mm pitch ([search summary of DIN 82 references, Scribd DIN 82 profile overview](https://www.scribd.com/doc/306264741/Knurling-profiles-DIN-82); [Zeus/Dieterle catalogue](https://www.dieterle-tools.de/files/dieterle/source/Dieterle/Kataloge/Standard-Aussen/Zeus_Technik_EN.pdf)). Note that on helical and diamond knurls, catalogue pitch is measured **perpendicular to the teeth**, not around the circumference ([Accu-Trak, general knurling information](https://accu-trak.com/general-knurling-information)).
- **Profile angle** — the included angle of the tooth V, typically 90° (105° also exists) ([Dieterle/Zeus catalogue](https://www.dieterle-tools.de/files/dieterle/source/Dieterle/Kataloge/Standard-Aussen/Zeus_Technik_EN.pdf)).
- **Depth versus pitch** — for a full (sharp) form, tooth depth is a fixed fraction of pitch set by the profile angle: **≈42% of pitch for a 90° form**, ≈48% for 80°, ≈55% for 70° ([Accu-Trak, general knurling information](https://accu-trak.com/general-knurling-information)). Production knurls are almost never full-sharp; crests are truncated or radiused, so real depth is less.
- **Pre-turn diameter** — rolled knurls displace material, so the blank is turned under the finished crest diameter by a pitch-dependent offset (e.g. d − 0.5·P for one family, d − 0.67·P for RGE diamond) ([Dieterle/Zeus catalogue](https://www.dieterle-tools.de/files/dieterle/source/Dieterle/Kataloge/Standard-Aussen/Zeus_Technik_EN.pdf)). For a CNC-milled knurl (which the 60's ring will be — anodised then diamond-cut, so the knurl is machined, not rolled) this offset is irrelevant, but the tooth-count constraint below still applies.
- **Tooth count must be an integer.** The circumference must be a whole multiple of the circumferential pitch or the pattern double-cuts: Diameter = (N × pitch) / π ([AIMS Industrial knurling guide](https://aimsindustrial.com.au/blogs/product-guides/knurling-guide)). On a milled knurl you choose N directly and the pitch falls out.

Pitch bands in general machining practice ([AIMS Industrial knurling guide](https://aimsindustrial.com.au/blogs/product-guides/knurling-guide), [ECOREPRAP complete guide to knurling](https://ecoreprap.com/blog/complete-guide-to-knurling/)):

- Fine: 0.5–0.6 mm (small parts, "easy on the hands and looks matter")
- Medium: 0.8–1.2 mm (parts roughly Ø10–50 mm)
- Coarse: 1.5–2.0 mm (large handles needing "serious grip")

The rule of thumb is "smaller diameter, finer pitch" — but the guides frame the upper end (1.2–2.0 mm) as being for tool handles gripped with the whole hand, not rings turned with fingertips. The AIMS guide explicitly says a 0.5 mm pitch on a Ø50 mm part reads "slippery and visually inconsistent" — too fine disappears; the pattern must stay legible at the object's scale.

On pattern choice, the same guide is direct: **straight knurl (RAA, the axial-groove type) is the pattern for knobs and rotary grips**; **diamond (RGE, the raised-point crossed helix) gives the strongest overall grip and dominates tool handles** ([AIMS Industrial knurling guide](https://aimsindustrial.com.au/blogs/product-guides/knurling-guide)). The PTSMAKE guide adds that four variables — pitch, angle, depth, tooth profile — fully define any knurl, and that on stainless a nominally 40-TPI (teeth-per-inch) roll can double-track to 80 TPI, i.e. execution matters as much as spec ([PTSMAKE ultimate guide to knurling patterns](https://www.ptsmake.com/the-ultimate-guide-to-expert-knurling-patterns/)).

## 2. Watches: bezels and crowns

The watch industry is the best library of "knurl you touch every day and love".

**Rolex Submariner.** The rotating bezel has 120 clicks per revolution and a knurled/scalloped edge for wet-hand grip ([Rolex Forums thread on the 120-click bezel](https://www.rolexforums.com/showthread.php?t=412610), [Bob's Watches Submariner bezel guide](https://www.bobswatches.com/rolex-blog/watch-101/rolex-submariner-bezels-ultimate-guide.html)). The relevant structure: **2 clicks per visible tooth group, and the grip features are large-scale scallops, not fine knurl** — a ~40 mm bezel uses coarse, widely spaced grip features because the finger needs purchase to overcome detent torque. No published Rolex spec for tooth count or depth was found; treat any figure you see quoted as unverified. Photo reference: the Bob's Watches guide above carries good macro photography of every bezel generation.

**Tudor Black Bay.** The most useful single data point in this strand, because Tudor changed the edge treatment and reviewers noticed. The original Black Bay 58 used a **coin-edge bezel** (fine straight reeding); the 2026 revision moved to a **"sharper, more defined knurling — 'toothier' than before"**, and reviewers describe the new edge as giving "immediate, positive grip" with a 60-click mechanism whose clicks are "firm, sure, and audible with little to no play" ([Monochrome Watches hands-on, revamped Black Bay 58](https://monochrome-watches.com/2026-tudor-black-bay-58-black-gilt-master-chronometer-metas-7939a1a0nu-review/), [Everest Bands hands-on with the new Black Bay 58 and 68](https://www.everestbands.com/blogs/bezel-barrel/hands-on-with-tudor-s-new-black-bay-58-and-68), [Exquisite Timepieces Black Bay 58 review](https://www.exquisitetimepieces.com/blog/tudor-black-bay-58-review/)). Read: coin-edge is the refined look; when a maker needs more torque authority against detents they coarsen the teeth, and the trade is explicitly aesthetic-versus-grip. The Black Bay's 60-click bezel is also the closest analogue in the survey to the 60's sixty detents.

**Crowns generally.** A knurled crown uses straight, angled, or crossed lines for grip; fluted/ridged variants define brand identity as much as function ([Oracle of Time, styles of watch crowns](https://oracleoftime.com/styles-of-watch-crowns/), [Ethos watch-crown guide](https://www.ethoswatches.com/the-watch-guide/watch-crown-types-shapes-uses/)). IWC's Big Pilot uses a large conical crown with "non-slip fluting" sized for gloved operation ([Chrono24 magazine on watch crowns](https://www.chrono24.com/magazine/watch-parts-in-focus-the-watch-crown-p_142302/)) — again, coarse features where torque and impaired grip demand it. One patent-literature data point: a wristwatch winding-stem grain knurl with **18 teeth** on a crown-scale part ([US patent via search on crown knurling](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11042122)) — at a typical ~Ø6–7 mm crown that is a circumferential pitch around 1 mm (derived, unverified against any specific commercial crown). No published tooth counts, depths or pitches were found for Hublot Big Bang, Panerai or Sinn — manufacturers do not publish them; anything circulating is enthusiast measurement. Marked here as **not found**, not as absent from the products.

**Clous de Paris** (hobnail): a grid of small pyramids engraved into the surface — geometrically the same solid as a raised-point diamond knurl, but presented as jewellery rather than grip, on dials and bezels (Patek Philippe Calatrava 6119, Piaget) ([Monochrome Watches glossary, clous de Paris](https://monochrome-watches.com/glossary/clous-de-paris/), [Collectability on the Patek hobnail bezel](https://collectability.com/learn/patek-philippe-iconic-design-clous-de-paris-hobnail-dial/), [SwissWatchExpo hobnail Calatrava guide](https://www.swisswatchexpo.com/thewatchclub/2026/01/02/patek-philippe-hobnail-calatrava-guide/)). The lesson for the 60: a fine, sharp-cornered pyramid grid at small pitch reads as ornament, not tooling — the diamond knurl can be the object's second piece of jewellery if the pitch is fine and the geometry crisp. Photo reference: the Time and Watches piece on the [Calatrava 6119 hobnail bezel](https://www.timeandwatches.com/2021/04/patek-philippe-calatrava-clous-de-paris.html) has close-ups.

## 3. Camera lens rings

Historic pattern: early lens rings used plain straight knurls; the milled "mountain and valley" scalloped grip appeared in the early 1950s specifically for focusing rings ([Flickr discussion of Leica Elmarit-R focusing ring history](https://www.flickr.com/groups/1373213@N25/discuss/72157628027241039/)). Leica's 1959 Summilux 50mm shipped with a scalloped focus ring, replaced by a knurled ring in the 1961 version 2, and the scalloped ring is what Leica reissued in the 2016 special edition — it is the collectible detail ([Slack & Co on the classic Summilux 50](https://www.slack.co.uk/articles/leica-classic-summilux-50mm.html), [Blue Moon Camera codex on identifying Leica M lenses](https://bluemooncameracodex.com/technical-reviews/2024/4/26/leica-m-lenses-amp-how-to-identify-them), [Steve Huff on the black chrome Summilux edition](https://www.stevehuffphoto.com/2016/01/11/a-look-at-the-leica-50-summilux-asph-black-chrome-special-edition/)).

The structural point: **a lens ring is turned tangentially with two fingertips — exactly the 60's gesture — and the lens industry converged on axial features (straight ribs or scallops), not diamond, because axial ridges maximise grip against the direction of rotation**. Fujifilm's milled camera dials show the interaction-design side: on the X-T5 the ISO dial and shutter dial are deliberately given different click weights (ISO looser with dull clicks, shutter stiffer with pronounced clicks) so the fingers can tell them apart without looking ([DPReview forum thread on X-T5 dial feel](https://www.dpreview.com/forums/thread/4683787)). No published knurl specs for Fujifilm or Zeiss rings were found — **not found**, unverified territory.

## 4. Pens, torches, audio and consumer knobs

**Rotring 600.** The canonical "precise" knurl. A fine diamond knurl on a ~Ø8 mm (estimated from product photos, unverified) metal grip. The consistent user judgment across decades of reviews: the grip is "secure enough that you didn't have to worry about losing the pencil but didn't make it feel like you were handling a cheese grater... you could write for quite some time without your fingertips getting sore" ([Gentleman Reviewer, Rotring 600 review](https://gentlemanreviewer.wordpress.com/2019/05/19/rotring-600-mechanical-pencil-review/), [Dave's Mechanical Pencils review](http://davesmechanicalpencils.blogspot.com/2010/02/rotring-600-mechanical-pencil-review.html), [rOtring official product page](https://www.rotring.com/pens-pencils/mechanical-pencils/rotring-600/SP_1532010.html)). The "cheese grater" line is the whole design brief in one phrase: the failure mode of knurling on a constantly handled object is not slip, it is abrasion.

**Surefire and tactical torches.** The opposite pole. Surefire's deeply cut, rolled knurling — crests left unshaped from rolling — is praised for wet/gloved grip and criticised for the same reason: "such knurling chews up pants-pockets" ([Candle Power Forums, "Aggressive knurling — yes or no?"](https://www.candlepowerforums.com/threads/aggressive-knurling-yes-or-no.207650/)). Surefire itself ships some models (E1B) deliberately smooth. Aggressive knurl is a tool-retention feature under adverse conditions; none of those conditions exist on a desk. The "Hexxagon" flashlight named in the brief could not be located under that name (searches return a puzzle game); the nearest survey equivalents are the machined-titanium EDC lights with helical/fine knurling, e.g. the Tactile Turn Apollo ([Tactile Turn Apollo product page](https://tactileturn.com/products/apollo)) and the knurl-for-heat-dissipation designs surveyed at [1Lumen's titanium flashlight roundup](https://1lumen.com/the-best-titanium-flashlights/). **Marked not found.**

**Audio.** The most relevant object in the entire survey is the **Naim Mu-so volume dial**: a large solid ring of bead-blasted anodised aluminium rotating around a fixed circular touch panel — the 60's architecture almost exactly. Reviewers: it "will attract endless fiddling purely for the fun of it", "spins around the central control section smoothly and effortlessly and feels great under the fingers", with a diffused light ring indicating volume ([What Hi-Fi Mu-so review](https://www.whathifi.com/naim/mu-so/review), [Trusted Reviews Mu-so review](https://www.trustedreviews.com/reviews/naim-audio-mu-so), [Sound & Vision Mu-so review](https://www.soundandvision.com/content/naim-mu-so-music-system-review)). Note what Naim did **not** do: no knurl at all — a bead-blasted smooth ring. The pleasure reviewers report comes from mass, bearing smoothness and the light, not from tooth grip. Chord's Mojo likewise builds its tactile identity on machined aluminium plus smooth glass balls, no knurl ([Stereophile Chord Mojo review](https://www.stereophile.com/content/chord-electronics-mojo-da-headphone-amplifier)). McIntosh and KEF LSX control specifics: **not found** at spec level.

**Teenage Engineering.** The TX-6 mixer's CNC-machined aluminium knobs are tiny but reviewers reach for the same reference the 60 does: "the whole thing has the density and feel of a luxury watch", knobs "satisfyingly precise... don't wiggle in the slightest" ([magazin Mehatronika TX-6 review](https://magazinmehatronika.com/en/teenage-engineering-tx-6-review/), [Root Nation TX-6 review](https://root-nation.com/en/audio-en/audio-equipment-en/en-teenage-engineering-tx-6-mixer-review/)). Precision is read from zero play and machining crispness before it is read from texture.

**Apple Digital Crown.** Described by Apple and press as a knurled knob whose feel deliberately quotes the mechanical watch crown while the detents are synthetic ([AppleInsider first look at the Digital Crown](https://forums.appleinsider.com/discussion/185973/first-look-digital-crown-on-apple-watch-ushers-in-new-era-of-ui-control), [Gear Patrol on the analog origins of the Digital Crown](https://www.gearpatrol.com/watches/a36097909/digital-crown-apple-watch/)). Its groove pitch and depth are unpublished — **unverified** — but the visible pattern is straight axial reeding (coin edge), not diamond, on a part turned with one fingertip. That is Apple solving the 60's exact gesture and choosing axial ridges.

**The coin edge itself** has a public spec, which makes it the one fully citable fine-reeding reference: the US Mint cuts **118 reeds on the dime (Ø17.91 mm), 119 on the quarter (Ø24.26 mm), 150 on the half dollar (Ø30.61 mm)** ([Mental Floss citing US Mint reeding counts](https://www.mentalfloss.com/article/28044/how-many-ridges-are-quarter-and-why-are-they-there-first-place), [Wikipedia, quarter specifications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarter_(United_States_coin))). Derived circumferential pitch: quarter ≈ 0.64 mm, half dollar ≈ 0.64 mm, dime ≈ 0.48 mm (arithmetic from the cited counts and diameters). So "coin edge", the texture everyone's fingers already know as *money-precise*, sits at **~0.5–0.65 mm pitch**. That is the calibration point for what pitch "reads as precise".

## 5. Synthesis: what pitch reads as precise, what reads as aggressive

Pulling the survey together:

- **~0.5–0.65 mm pitch** (coin reeding, fine straight knurl): reads as currency/instrument precision. Risk at Ø134: the AIMS warning that too-fine on a large diameter looks inconsistent and grips poorly ([AIMS Industrial knurling guide](https://aimsindustrial.com.au/blogs/product-guides/knurling-guide)) — though that warning is written for grip-critical tool work, not ornament.
- **~0.8–1.2 mm pitch** (DIN 82's common RGE 0.8, medium band): reads as engineered, Rotring-class. This is the band the machining guides assign to Ø10–50 mm parts; on Ø134 it is proportionally fine, which pushes it toward "precise" rather than "grippy".
- **≥1.5 mm pitch, full-sharp 90° form**: reads as tactical/tool (Surefire), with documented abrasion complaints ([Candle Power Forums aggressive-knurling thread](https://www.candlepowerforums.com/threads/aggressive-knurling-yes-or-no.207650/)).
- **Depth is the aggression dial more than pitch.** Full-sharp form at 90° is ≈0.42×pitch deep ([Accu-Trak](https://accu-trak.com/general-knurling-information)); every "comfortable" example in the survey truncates or radiuses the crests (Rotring; AIMS advises softening fresh crests with 400-grit ([AIMS guide](https://aimsindustrial.com.au/blogs/product-guides/knurling-guide))). Sharp full-depth diamond points are what fingers file complaints about.
- **Grip direction matters.** Objects turned tangentially with fingertips (lens rings, bezels, the Digital Crown, coin edges) converge on **axial features** — straight ribs, scallops, coin reeding — because ridges perpendicular to the motion resist it best; diamond knurl is the omnidirectional pattern for shafts gripped in a fist ([AIMS guide: straight for knobs, diamond for tool handles](https://aimsindustrial.com.au/blogs/product-guides/knurling-guide)).

**Knurl-to-edge transitions, best practice.** Machining references agree on three points: leave a **0.5–1 mm plain (unknurled) band at each end** of a knurled zone so displaced/cut material never forms a raised burr at the boundary ([ECOREPRAP complete guide to knurling](https://ecoreprap.com/blog/complete-guide-to-knurling/)); define the knurl width with a relief and chamfer the ends ([Kitplanes home-shop machinist column](https://www.kitplanes.com/home-shop-machinist-36/)); where a rolled knurl must start from an edge, a ~30° lead-in at reduced diameter beats 45° ([JEELIX knurling handbook](https://www.jeelix.com/knurling-handbook/)) — though this last point concerns roll-forming loads and does not bind a milled knurl. The watch-world version of the same idea is visible on every good bezel: the textured band is bordered top and bottom by clean turned surfaces, and the texture dies into a defined groove or step, never directly into a polished face.

## Recommendations for the 60

All three assume the knurl is CNC-milled (not rolled), since the ring is anodised and then diamond-cut — a rolled knurl before anodising would be possible but the tooth-count and burr control below are easier milled. Diameter basis: Ø134 mm → circumference 421.0 mm (arithmetic).

1. **Side knurl: raised-point diamond in the style of RGE per DIN 82 (the German knurl standard's crossed-helix, raised-point diamond), 30° helix, 420 points around the circumference → circumferential pitch 1.00 mm, i.e. exactly 7 diamond columns per detent of the sixty.** Locking the tooth count to an integer multiple of 60 means the texture is phase-locked to the detents — the pattern and the click grid never beat against each other, and a detent always lands on the same tooth phase. 1.0 mm sits in the documented medium band (0.8–1.2 mm, [AIMS Industrial](https://aimsindustrial.com.au/blogs/product-guides/knurling-guide)), on the "precise" side of the survey's precise/aggressive line, and stays legible at Ø134 where a 0.5 mm coin pitch risks reading as a matt blur. Profile angle 90° (the DIN 82 default per the [Dieterle/Zeus catalogue](https://www.dieterle-tools.de/files/dieterle/source/Dieterle/Kataloge/Standard-Aussen/Zeus_Technik_EN.pdf)).

2. **Tooth depth 0.28–0.30 mm — deliberately truncated from the theoretical full-sharp depth — with flatted or ~0.05 mm radiused crests (radius value estimated, to be tuned on the sample).** Full-sharp 90° form at 1.0 mm pitch would be ≈0.42 mm deep ([Accu-Trak depth ratios](https://accu-trak.com/general-knurling-information)); cutting to ~70% of that and killing the crest points is exactly the difference the survey found between Rotring 600 ("secure... not a cheese grater", [Gentleman Reviewer](https://gentlemanreviewer.wordpress.com/2019/05/19/rotring-600-mechanical-pencil-review/)) and Surefire ("chews up pants-pockets", [Candle Power Forums](https://www.candlepowerforums.com/threads/aggressive-knurling-yes-or-no.207650/)). The 60 is touched hundreds of times a day; abrasion, not slip, is the failure mode. The exact depth must be validated on a machined test ring — mark it V (unverified) in CAD until then.

3. **Transitions: end the knurl in a plain turned band of 0.8 mm (within the 0.5–1 mm range machining references give, [ECOREPRAP](https://ecoreprap.com/blog/complete-guide-to-knurling/)) before the coin-edged rim above and before the 1.2 mm lower chamfer, with the band's floor at the knurl's root diameter, not its crest diameter.** Setting the plain band at root depth means the diamond crests stand proud of a recessed clean line — the knurl reads as a raised, bordered texture panel (the watch-bezel grammar) and no cut tooth ever terminates raggedly into the diamond-cut chamfer or the reeded rim. Cut order matters: knurl and reeding milled before anodising; the diamond-cut pass on the 45° chamfer after anodising must not touch the knurled zone, which the plain band guarantees with 0.8 mm of margin. Secondary: give the coin-edged rim a reed count that is also a multiple of 60 (e.g. 600 reeds → 0.70 mm pitch, derived arithmetic; near the quarter's 0.64 mm money-precise pitch, [US Mint reeding counts](https://www.mentalfloss.com/article/28044/how-many-ridges-are-quarter-and-why-are-they-there-first-place)) so both textures share the detent grid.

## Possibly wrong

1. **Diamond knurl on the side of a fingertip-rotated ring is the survey's outlier choice.** Every surveyed object that shares the 60's gesture — tangential fingertip rotation — uses axial features: lens rings (scallops/straight ribs, [Blue Moon Camera codex](https://bluemooncameracodex.com/technical-reviews/2024/4/26/leica-m-lenses-amp-how-to-identify-them)), dive bezels (coin edge or coarse teeth, [Monochrome Watches on the Black Bay](https://monochrome-watches.com/2026-tudor-black-bay-58-black-gilt-master-chronometer-metas-7939a1a0nu-review/)), the Apple Digital Crown (straight reeding, [AppleInsider](https://forums.appleinsider.com/discussion/185973/first-look-digital-crown-on-apple-watch-ushers-in-new-era-of-ui-control)), coins themselves. Diamond is the pattern for fist-gripped shafts ([AIMS guide](https://aimsindustrial.com.au/blogs/product-guides/knurling-guide)). The decision stands — and the clous-de-Paris tradition shows fine diamond can be jewellery ([Monochrome glossary](https://monochrome-watches.com/glossary/clous-de-paris/)) — but be aware the 60 is choosing the ornamental reading over the ergonomic one, and the coin-edged rim above the knurl is then doing the actual rotational-grip work. If the sample ring feels slippery under light fingertips, the fix is not deeper diamonds; it is leaning on the rim's axial reeding.

2. **Two adjacent textures (diamond side + coin-edge rim) risk visual noise on one small ring.** No source states this as a rule; it is a judgment call flagged for the sample review. The precedent that works is the watch bezel, where the two textures are separated by a clean turned line — which recommendation 3's recessed plain band provides. If the sample looks busy, the cheaper texture to delete is the coin edge, not the knurl — but see point 1: it may be the one earning its keep.

3. **No other error found in the product context.** The 45° diamond-cut chamfer, the small inner chamfer, and the 1.2 mm lower chamfer are all consistent with surveyed practice; the 30°-lead-in advice in machining references ([JEELIX handbook](https://www.jeelix.com/knurling-handbook/)) applies to roll-knurling entry loads and does not argue against a 45° decorative chamfer on a milled part.

## Gaps and honesty notes

- Manufacturer tooth counts, pitches and depths for Rolex, Tudor, Hublot, Panerai, IWC, Sinn, Leica, Fujifilm, Zeiss, Surefire, Naim, McIntosh, KEF and Apple are **not published**; nothing in this document presents one as fact. Where a count exists it is either a click count (Rolex 120, Tudor 60) or a government spec (US Mint reeding).
- The "Hexxagon" flashlight could not be identified; searches return an unrelated puzzle game.
- The full DIN 82 pitch series table sits behind the paywalled standard; the pitches quoted here (0.6 RAA, 0.8 RGE, 90°/105° profiles) come from tool-maker catalogues implementing it. Buying the standard (or IS 3403:1981, the free Indian mirror, linked above as PDF) is worth doing before the drawing is issued.
- All Ø134 tooth-count arithmetic (420 teeth → 1.00 mm, 600 reeds → 0.70 mm) is derivation, not measurement.
