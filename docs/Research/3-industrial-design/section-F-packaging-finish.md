# Section F — Packaging, unboxing and finish

Research strand F for the 60 by Cadrane: (1) unboxing as a possession ritual for a 60-unit numbered run, and (2) durability of the anodised and diamond-cut finish under daily fingertip contact. Every figure below either carries a source link or is marked "quote required" / "unverified". Written 2026-09-02.

---

## F1. Unboxing as a possession ritual

### F1.1 Leica (cameras)

Leica's limited editions (small numbered runs, exactly the situation of the 60) carry an edition number alongside the regular serial number, ship in packaging distinct from the standard product's box, and include a certificate of authenticity. The [apotelyt survey of Leica special editions](https://apotelyt.com/photo-camera/leica-special-edition) describes the pattern: elaborate box-packaging, special engravings marking the occasion, and edition numbering. The [Leica M Edition 70](https://leicacamerausa.com/leica-m-edition-70.html) (250 sets worldwide) ships with special-edition packaging and a certificate of authenticity. A recent unboxing of the [Leica M11 "100 Years of Leica – Dubai" edition, camera number 35 of 100](https://leicarumors.com/2025/01/26/leica-m11-100-years-of-leica-dubai-uae-limited-edition-camera-unboxing-and-quick-review.aspx/) (page includes unboxing photos) shows the mechanism clearly: a plain protective outer shipping box, a distinct presentation box inside, the number printed on the certificate rather than shouted on the outside.

The transferable pattern: **the number lives on the certificate and an engraving, not on the packaging graphics**. The box is distinct from the standard product's box but still restrained.

### F1.2 Bang & Olufsen (hi-fi)

B&O's current packaging is singled out by reviewers for two things: no plastic, and cloth. The [Homes & Gardens review of the Beosound Premiere](https://www.homesandgardens.com/solved/bang-olufsen-beosound-premiere-review) calls the unboxing "easy and luxurious, with no plastic anywhere", with every item "presented in soft material bags in a way that feels entirely in keeping with the high price tag" — and separately criticises the box for being too heavy for one person to lift. That is the ceremony/waste line in one review: cloth bags read as ceremony; bulk and weight read as burden. The [T3 review](https://www.t3.com/tech/soundbars/bang-and-olufsen-beosound-premiere-review) makes the same luxury framing. Note B&O ships full accessory sets (HDMI, LAN, mains cables, polishing cloth) — the polishing cloth is a cheap item that reviewers reliably mention, relevant to a fingerprint-prone black aluminium product.

### F1.3 Teenage Engineering (electronic instruments)

The most-copied recent example in hardware. The OP-1 field synthesiser ships in a recycled-cardboard box with monochrome print whose inner tray doubles as a travel case, held by elastic straps — [gearnews review](https://www.gearnews.com/teenage-engineering-op-1-field-review/) ("premium... almost as though it was made with unboxing videos in mind... the box is designed to double as a transport case"). The moulded trays are biodegradable moulded paper pulp by [PaperFoam, who publish a case study with photos of the triple-tray design](https://www.paperfoam.com/portfolio/teenage-engineering-op-1/). In the box: the unit, "a nice thick USB-C cable", a small manual, and velcro pads ([Sound On Sound review](https://www.soundonsound.com/reviews/teenage-engineering-op-1-field)). Two lessons for the 60: a moulded pulp tray can read as premium if the fit is exact, and a deliberately good USB-C cable gets noticed — a thin generic one would be noticed too, the wrong way.

Images: the [PaperFoam case-study page](https://www.paperfoam.com/portfolio/teenage-engineering-op-1/) carries good photographs of the tray set; [unboxing video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QVc18vb3p8w).

### F1.4 Watch houses

- **Omega and Rolex** — On the WatchUSeek forum thread ["Nicest watch presentation boxes you have received?"](https://forums.watchuseek.com/f2/nicest-watch-presentation-boxes-you-have-received-4783809.html) and the thread on [Omega wood boxes](https://www.watchuseek.com/threads/what-do-you-do-with-your-wood-omega-presentation-boxes.5302269/), owners rate Omega's current wooden boxes highly ("so nice that owners hesitate to store them in closets") and note Rolex ships protective extras such as bezel protectors. The same threads carry the waste complaint verbatim: some owners say they would prefer a pouch and a lower price. Big-box ceremony divides even the customers it is aimed at.
- **Grand Seiko** — a cautionary example: the [WatchUSeek thread on Grand Seiko packaging](https://www.watchuseek.com/threads/the-gs-watch-box-and-packaging.4998889/) records owners calling the box underwhelming for the price, using similar plastic-and-cardboard packaging regardless of price point. A weak box does register against a strong product.
- **anOrdain** (Glasgow, small independent — closest analogue to Cadrane in scale) — the most instructive case. The [Worn & Wound review of the Model 1](https://wornandwound.com/review/review-anordain-model-1/) and [The Time Bum review](https://www.thetimebum.com/2022/01/anordain-model-1.html) describe: a plain recyclable cardboard shipping box; inside, an Ordnance-Survey-map-inspired printed wrap, a black-and-gold booklet telling the company's story and naming the artisans, and a leather travel case (sourced from Florence) instead of a display box. The reviews name the suppliers: outer box by W MacCarthy & Sons (London box maker), wrap paper by Arjowiggins in Scotland. A photographer's [unboxing photo essay](https://www.erwinhartenbergphoto.com/post/unboxing-the-anordain-model-1-watch-a-photoshoot) shows the full sequence with good images. Separately, [Clyde Presentation Packaging (Scotland) publish a case study on making boxes for anOrdain](https://www.clydepresentationpackaging.com/case-study/exquisite-box-designs-for-premium-watchmakers/) — a named UK short-run supplier with a directly relevant reference customer. Prices: quote required (none published).
- **Christopher Ward** (UK, mid-priced) — reviewers ([Ben's Watch Club](https://www.benswatchclub.com/blog/christopher-ward-watches-review), [Exquisite Timepieces](https://www.exquisitetimepieces.com/blog/christopher-ward-watches-review/)) describe a compact bamboo/polyurethane/cardboard box, "comparable to far more expensive brands", explicitly compact ("doesn't take up a lot of space"). Their own [Trustpilot reviews](https://uk.trustpilot.com/review/christopherward.com) repeatedly mention the presentation box — evidence that packaging is one of the few things ordinary buyers spontaneously review.

### F1.5 What reads as ceremony, what reads as waste

Across the sources above, the items reviewers single out positively: the certificate/number, a booklet with the story and the people, cloth or leather that outlives the unboxing (B&O bags, anOrdain travel case), a tray that has a second life (Teenage Engineering), the absence of plastic, and a good cable. What draws complaints: sheer box size and weight (B&O), boxes too nice to use but too big to keep (Omega thread), and packaging that undershoots the price (Grand Seiko thread).

## F2. Finish durability

### F2.1 Anodising types — MIL-A-8625, the US military anodising specification

MIL-A-8625, the US military specification for anodic coatings on aluminium, is the reference everyone quotes. Per the [Aluminum Anodizers Council summary](https://www.anodizing.org/military-specification-mil-a-8625/): Type I is chromic acid anodise; Type II is conventional sulfuric acid anodise; Type III is hardcoat. Class 1 is undyed, Class 2 is dyed. Coating thickness must be called out on the drawing rather than assumed.

[Anoplate's hardcoat page](https://www.anoplate.com/finishes/hardcoat-anodize/) gives the working numbers: Type II conventional sulfuric anodise runs 0.0002–0.0006 inch (roughly 5–15 µm) thick; Type III hardcoat runs 0.0005–0.0030 inch (roughly 12–75 µm) and is produced in a colder bath at higher current density, giving a harder, denser, more abrasion-resistant layer. Alloy choice matters: per [Anoplate](https://www.anoplate.com/finishes/hardcoat-anodize/), 6xxx-series alloys (the usual CNC choices, 6061/6082) hardcoat to a uniform deep grey-black, while 2xxx and 7xxx series trend bronze-grey and can show colour variation from copper/zinc content.

For a knob handled every day, the practical difference: Type II dyed black relies on dye held in sealed pores of a thin coating; Type III is thick enough and hard enough that the colour is substantially the oxide itself.

### F2.2 Colours and fingerprints

- **Type III colour range is narrow by nature.** Per [Anoplate](https://www.anoplate.com/finishes/hardcoat-anodize/) and the [RivCut Type II vs Type III comparison](https://www.rivcut.com/blog/anodizing-type-comparison), hardcoat's dense, fine-pored oxide resists dye uptake; realistic colours are natural (dark grey/charcoal on 6xxx alloy), black over-dye, and dark bronze. Bright colours come out muddy. For the 60's palette — matt black, dark grey, natural — Type III's limitation costs nothing: the entire desired palette is exactly what hardcoat does natively.
- **Matt texture is made before anodising, not by it.** The [finishing.com forum thread on optical matte black anodise](https://www.finishing.com/145/45.shtml) (practitioner answers, not marketing) is clear that the matt surface comes from pre-treatment — caustic etch, or vapour/fine-grit bead blast — and warns that over-etching produces a blotchy "spackled" finish, so the etch/blast recipe needs test panels agreed with the anodiser before production. Reported achievable reflectivity on matte black anodise is 0.05–0.3 (same thread).
- **Fingerprints on matt black anodise.** Sources agree matt hides fingerprints far better than gloss: a matte etched/blasted surface scatters light so oil films don't show as sharp prints — [Runsom black anodising guide](https://www.runsom.com/blog/black-anodizing/), [Pioneer Metal Finishing on black finishes](https://www.pioneermetal.com/blog/pioneer-metal-finishing-black-finishes-for-aluminum-products-black-is-not-always-just-black/). "Hides" is not "immune": sealed anodise is non-porous, so skin oil sits on the surface and wipes off, but a very fine blast texture will still show smudges under raking light. No quantified fingerprint-visibility data was found from an independent source; treat degree-of-smudging as something to judge on the anodiser's test panels. Shipping a polishing cloth (as Bang & Olufsen do, section F1.2) is the honest mitigation.
- **Dye lightfastness.** For dyed (Class 2) black, the [finishing.com thread](https://www.finishing.com/145/45.shtml) recommends inorganic black dyes or electrolytic colouring for lightfastness. A desk object sits in daylight for years; specify a lightfast black, not the cheapest organic dye.

### F2.3 Diamond-cut after anodise — the bright chamfer

- **The process is real and documented.** Apple's patent [US 9,338,908, "Electronic devices with reflective chamfer surfaces"](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9338908) describes exactly the 60's intended feature: rough-cut the chamfer, anodise the part, then finish-cut through the anodise with a single-crystal diamond tool to expose a bright specular ring against the dark coating. [Diamond turning generally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_turning) produces specular surfaces (roughness on the order of 10 nm) needing no polishing. The same technique is standard on alloy wheels — [Ronal Group's diamond-cut page](https://www.ronalgroup.com/en/technologies2/diamond-cut/) (with photos): pre-coated wheel, face turned with a diamond tool to expose bright metal.
- **The cautionary tale is the iPhone 5.** The slate/black iPhone 5 paired dark anodise with diamond-cut chamfers; the chamfer and edges scuffed to bright metal in normal use, and about 47% of polled owners reported scratches out of the box or shortly after — ["Scuffgate" coverage at MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/2012/09/21/black-iphone-5-anodized-aluminum-susceptible-to-scratching/), [VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/mobile/iphone-5-scratches). Two readings for the 60: first, thin dyed anodise on sharp edges wears through and shows bright metal — on the 60 the bright ring is deliberate, so the classic failure is partly designed out; second, the *anodised* black areas adjacent to the cut still scuff, and on black the contrast makes every scuff visible. This argues for hardcoat on the handled ring rather than thin Type II.
- **The bare bright surface must be protected.** Freshly diamond-cut aluminium is unprotected metal. The alloy-wheel industry's experience is unambiguous: the cut face is sealed under clear lacquer, and where the lacquer is breached, filiform ("white worm") corrosion creeps underneath — [AlloyHub on diamond-cut lacquer and why it fails](https://www.alloyhub.com/diamond-cut-alloy-wheel-lacquer/), [Manchester Powder Coating on oxidation in diamond-cut wheels](https://manchesterpowdercoating.co.uk/blog/oxidation-and-kerb-damage-in-diamond-cut-wheels/). A desk object sees no road salt, but it does see skin salts and oil daily on exactly that ring. Options: (a) thin clear lacquer over the cut (wheel practice); (b) a second, clear anodise pass after cutting so the bright ring is sealed under transparent oxide — this "double anodise" sequence is the watch/consumer-electronics approach implied by the Apple patent flow, but no supplier document confirming a second-anodise step for a specific product was found: unverified — confirm the sequence with the anodiser. Doing nothing means the ring slowly dulls and fingerprints etch it.
- **Who does this in the UK.** No UK job shop was found advertising "diamond-cut bright chamfer after anodise" as a named service for small parts; this is a process to specify across two suppliers (machinist + anodiser) rather than buy off a menu. Candidates: [Metal Finishings Ltd](https://www.metalfinishingsltd.co.uk/treatments/hard-anodising/) (London, hard anodising); [Protolabs UK](https://www.protolabs.com/en-gb/services/secondary-services/anodising/) (CNC + anodise under one roof, useful for prototypes); the UK's many diamond-cutting wheel-refurbishment lathes prove the tooling is common in-country, but wheel shops handle wheels, not Ø135 mm knobs. All pricing: quote required. The realistic route is a precision machinist willing to take anodised parts back onto a lathe with a diamond insert, with fixturing that protects the finished anodise — raise as a supplier-development task in `docs/SOURCING-BOM.md`.

## Recommendations for the 60

1. **Package like anOrdain, not like Omega.** A compact short-run rigid box (Clyde Presentation Packaging or Showcase Creative, both UK — quote required), a letterpress-numbered certificate on cotton stock ("No. 07/60") from a UK studio such as Blush Publishing (quote required), a small booklet naming the people and process, a good USB-C cable, a polishing cloth, no plastic anywhere. Skip the oversized display box: the review evidence (sections F1.4–F1.5) says compact-and-considered beats big-and-lavish, and at 60 units every box can be hand-finished.
2. **Specify the handled ring as Type III hardcoat per MIL-A-8625 (the US military anodising specification), Class 1 undyed or Class 2 lightfast black, on a 6xxx alloy, with matte texture set by a bead-blast recipe agreed on test panels.** The 60's palette (matt black / dark grey / natural) is exactly hardcoat's native range, so the durability upgrade over Type II sulfuric anodise costs nothing aesthetically, and the iPhone 5 scuffgate history (section F2.3) shows what thin dyed Type II does on a handled black edge. Approve fingerprint behaviour on the physical test panels under raking light.
3. **Decide the bright chamfer's sealing step now, before CAD freeze.** Diamond-cut-after-anodise is proven (Apple patent US 9,338,908), but the cut ring is bare metal and will dull and stain under daily fingertips unless sealed — clear lacquer (wheel practice) or a second clear anodise pass (unverified for small parts; confirm with the anodiser). The choice affects process order, cost, and the exact brightness of the ring, and no UK shop sells this as a catalogue service, so it needs a named supplier pair and quotes in `docs/SOURCING-BOM.md`.

## Possibly wrong

1. **"Diamond-cut after anodising" as stated in the product context is incomplete as a process description.** The cut exposes bare aluminium; without a sealing step (clear lacquer or a second clear anodise) the bright ring oxidises, dulls, and takes fingerprint etching — the alloy-wheel industry documents this failure mode extensively ([AlloyHub](https://www.alloyhub.com/diamond-cut-alloy-wheel-lacquer/)). The context sentence reads as if the diamond cut is the final operation; on a daily-handled product it should not be.
2. **"Matt black anodise" plus a chamfer cut implies Type II dyed anodise by default, and that is the weaker choice for a part gripped every day.** The iPhone 5 in slate — dark Type II-style anodise with diamond-cut edges — scuffed to bright metal in normal use ([MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/2012/09/21/black-iphone-5-anodized-aluminum-susceptible-to-scratching/)). Nothing in the product context forbids Type III hardcoat, but it should be stated, because the two look similar on day one and behave differently in year three.
3. No other statement in the supplied product context conflicts with the evidence found.
