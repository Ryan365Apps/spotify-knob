# Section D — Light as the only colour

Research for the 60's downward-firing light ring: the 324° ring of discrete LEDs (light-emitting diodes) under the base, with a 36° opaque gap at the back for the sockets. Working assumption throughout: LED pitch ~4 mm (unverified, per the product brief). Every number below is either cited or marked unverified / rule of thumb.

---

## 1. How the reference products actually do it

**Amazon Echo Dot, 4th generation — the closest precedent to the 60.** Amazon moved the Alexa light ring from the top edge of the speaker to its base, "casting a nifty-looking glow on the surface upon which it's sitting" — the LEDs fire downward and the visible effect is the reflection off the desk, exactly the 60's scheme ([TechHive review](https://techhive.com/article/3586357/amazon-echo-dot-4th-gen-review.html), [Tom's Guide review](https://www.tomsguide.com/reviews/amazon-echo-dot)). Reviewers called the reflected glow easier to read than the old top ring. This is evidence the downward-firing decision is sound in principle — Amazon made the same move deliberately on a mass-market product and kept it for the 5th generation.

**Amazon Echo / Echo Dot internals.** The earlier top-ring Echo Dot uses 12 RGB (red-green-blue) LEDs driven by a 36-channel constant-current driver, with a white solder mask painted on the PCB (printed circuit board) edge to reflect light up into the diffuser, and a plastic diffuser ring over the LEDs ([Microcontroller Tips teardown](https://www.microcontrollertips.com/teardown-inside-amazons-echo-dot/), [iFixit Echo teardown, with photos](https://www.ifixit.com/Teardown/Amazon+Echo+Teardown/33953), [Brian Dorey Echo Dot 5th gen teardown, with photos](https://www.briandorey.com/post/echo-dot-5th-gen-smart-speaker-teardown)). Two transferable details: the white reflective mask under the LEDs (free efficiency), and an ambient light sensor that scales ring brightness to the room ([Predictable Designs teardown](https://predictabledesigns.com/product-development-teardown-of-an-amazon-echo-dot/)).

**Apple HomePod, 1st generation.** The top "display" is 19 RGB LEDs under a translucent dome-plus-diffuser; the deliberately blurry Siri orb is the diffuser doing its job — Apple chose heavy diffusion and low resolution over a crisp view of the emitters ([9to5Mac teardown summary](https://9to5mac.com/2018/02/12/homepod-teardown/), [iFixit HomePod teardown, with photos](https://www.ifixit.com/Teardown/HomePod+Teardown/103133)). The +/− volume icons each get their own LED triad plus a light guide "to aim the photons where they're needed" — precedent for using light guides where light must land precisely.

**Google's light-ring patents.** Google holds a family of patents specifically on making a uniform ring from a discrete number of LEDs: [US10914430, "Smart-home device light rings with lens spacing for uniform output"](https://patents.google.com/patent/US10914430B2/en) (assignee Google LLC — a light guide whose thickness tapers away from each LED, with micro-lens density increasing away from the LED, to flatten the brightness profile), plus siblings on [tapered sections (US11873952)](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11873952) and [scalloped transmissive sections (US11306878)](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11306878). The lesson is not to copy the mechanism; it is that a perfectly uniform ring from few LEDs costs real optical engineering. The 60's ~4 mm pitch (unverified) is dense enough to avoid needing any of it — see section 3.

**Google Nest Learning Thermostat.** The Nest's ring is not a light: it is the physical rotating metal bezel, and the 4th generation replaced polished stainless with matte brushed metal around a borderless circular display ([TechHive 4th-gen review](https://www.techhive.com/article/2442945/nest-learning-thermostat-4th-gen-review.html), [Google Store product page, with images](https://store.google.com/product/nest_learning_thermostat_4th_gen?hl=en-US)). Its relevant habit is the ambient light sensor dimming the display at night. The Nest is a "full ring" only in the mechanical sense — it is not evidence for or against a full ring of light.

**Xbox Series X.** The famous green vent glow is not a light at all: it is a layer of green plastic under the perforated top, angled so it reads as an inner glow ([Windows Central](https://www.windowscentral.com/xbox-series-x-looks-gorgeous-leds-we-dont-recommend-it), [ResetEra discussion](https://www.resetera.com/threads/series-x-green-glow-paying-homage-to-the-og-xbox.302885/)). Microsoft judged that a permanent real light would be a distraction in a dark room. Two lessons for the 60: (a) a static colour effect can be done in material, but the 60's design law (colour only from light, metal monochrome) rightly forbids it — a painted glow cannot change meaning; (b) a light that cannot be dimmed or turned off is a liability on a desk. The 60's ring must be dimmable to zero.

**Philips Hue Play Gradient Lightstrip.** The best-documented consumer "wash a surface with mixed light" product. Philips uses roughly three times the LED density of a normal strip plus a *double* diffuser so that "even if the TV is placed close to the wall, you will not see individual light points on the wall," and recommends 10–15 cm of projection distance to the wall for full colour blending ([Hueblog on wall distance](https://hueblog.com/2020/09/28/gradient-lightstrip-and-the-wall-distance-15-centimetres-should-be-enough/), [Hueblog review, with photos](https://hueblog.com/2020/10/05/my-review-philips-hue-play-gradient-lightstrip/)). Note the structure of the solution: density + diffusion + throw distance, all three at once. The 60 has high density (4 mm pitch, unverified) but very little throw distance (the ring sits ~a few mm to a couple of cm above the desk), so the diffuser and the LED-to-diffuser standoff have to do the work.

**Sonos Era 100.** The opposite pole of restraint: a single small status LED on top of the grille, with an app toggle to disable it ([Sonos status light support page](https://support.sonos.com/en-us/article/enable-or-disable-the-status-light), [Android Police review](https://www.androidpolice.com/sonos-era-100-review/)). Sonos treats light as an indicator, not a feature. The 60 sits between Sonos (light = status dot) and Echo (light = expressive ring); its design law — colour is ownership: green = volume, amber = seek, white = the device, red = mute and recording — is closer to Echo's colour-coded ring language (blue = listening, red = mic muted, yellow = notification, per the [CNN Underscored Echo Dot review](https://edition.cnn.com/cnn-underscored/reviews/amazon-echo-dot-4th-generation-review)).

**Teenage Engineering OB-4.** Almost no decorative light: the documented LED is a charging indicator that turns red while charging and off when done ([official OB-4 guide](https://teenage.engineering/guides/ob-4)). The product communicates state mechanically (the visible tape reels). Supports the general principle that on a premium object every lit element must mean something — which the 60's "colour is ownership" law already encodes.

## 2. Diffuser materials — the actual numbers

The two figures that matter are total light transmission (how much gets through) and hiding power (haze / diffusion angle — how completely the discrete emitters vanish). They trade off directly: "more scattering particles mean better hiding power but lower transmission"; the correct method is to fix the mechanical design (standoff distance and LED pitch) first, then choose the *lightest* diffusion grade that fully hides the array ([Hexatron, opal diffuser sheets compared](https://hexatrontech.com/information-hub/opal-diffuser-sheets-led-lighting/)).

### Opal PMMA (acrylic) — the benchmark

From the manufacturer's technical datasheet [PLEXIGLAS Satinice, Ref-No. 211-11](https://www.plexiglas.de/files/plexiglas-content/pdf/technische-informationen/211-11-EN-PLEXIGLAS-Satinice.pdf) (POLYVANTIS; sold as ACRYLITE in the Americas), typical values at 3 mm thickness, DIN 5036:

| Grade | Type | Transmission (TD65) |
|---|---|---|
| 0F00 SC/DC | clear, satin surface | 92 % |
| 0D010 DF | white translucent, diffuser beads throughout | 83 % |
| WH10 DC | white, satin both sides | 70 % |
| WH02 SC/DC | white | 44 % |
| WD300 DF | white, beads throughout (tube grade) | 21 %, half-value angle 56° |

The DF grades carry light-diffusing beads distributed *through* the material, not just a surface texture — the datasheet names 0D010 DF as the grade for lamp covers specifically. Satinice DF tubes come in Ø50–Ø200 mm with 3 mm walls, which brackets the 60's ring diameter — a stock extruded tube could conceivably be cut into the diffuser ring. [PERSPEX Diffuse](https://www.display.3acomposites.com/perspex/perspex-light/diffuse/) (3A Composites) offers the same idea as a graded system: opal grades at 60 %, 70 %, 80 % and 90 % transmission, chosen against LED pitch and cavity depth. PMMA (acrylic) is the optical benchmark: highest transmission for a given hiding power and the best UV (ultraviolet) stability — it holds its white point for years ([Hexatron comparison](https://hexatrontech.com/information-hub/opal-diffuser-sheets-led-lighting/)).

### Frosted polycarbonate — tougher, slightly less efficient

From the Covestro datasheet [Makrolon Lumen XT, PDS112](https://www.eplastics.com/pdf/PDS112_Lumen.pdf) (transmission to ASTM D 1003; diffusion as FWHM, the full width at half maximum of the scattered beam):

| Grade | Diffusion FWHM @1.5 mm / 3 mm | Transmission @1.5 mm / 3 mm |
|---|---|---|
| LC0 | 14° / 13° | 91 % / 90 % |
| LC5 | 53° / 77° | 88 % / 77 % |
| LC7 | 86° / 135° | 79 % / 62 % |
| LW9 | 146° / 166° | 73 % / 55 % |

This table is the clearest published statement of the trade: to get emitter-hiding diffusion (FWHM above ~130°) at 3 mm you give up 38–45 % of the light. Polycarbonate buys impact strength and temperature range over PMMA (acrylic) at slightly lower transmission ([Hexatron](https://hexatrontech.com/information-hub/opal-diffuser-sheets-led-lighting/)); for a ring buried under a steel base, impact strength is irrelevant, so PMMA wins unless moulding constraints say otherwise.

### 3D-printed translucent PETG — prototype only

PETG (the printable copolyester, glycol-modified PET) prints translucent and diffuses adequately when printed in vase mode with fuzzy skin ([MakerWorld example model](https://makerworld.com/en/models/886704-neck-light-led-diffuser-translucent-petg-pla)), but no manufacturer publishes transmission or haze figures for printed parts — the values depend on layer height, cooling and speed, and layer lines remain visible in transmitted light unless printed slowly with minimal cooling ([Bambu Lab forum, transparent PC diffuser thread](https://forum.bambulab.com/t/led-diffuser-using-transparent-pc/185469)). Right for bench-rig iterations of the ring geometry; wrong for the 60-unit run, where the diffuser is a visible lit surface and layer lines would read as texture in the glow.

## 3. Avoiding hot-spotting from discrete LEDs

**The pitch rule.** The most-cited quantitative rule: the LED-to-diffuser distance should equal the LED pitch — "if the LED pitch is 10 mm, the distance between the LEDs and the cover should also be 10 mm" ([Ledrise, LED pitch and distance for optimum backlighting](https://www.ledrise.eu/blog/led-pitch-and-distance-to-cover-for-optimum-backlighting/)). Closer than 1:1 and individual emitters show; farther and uniformity improves but light (and enclosure depth) is spent. Evo-Lite states the qualitative version — more distance means better diffusion, and if the application can't afford depth you need a stronger diffuser or a denser source ([Evo-Lite, the science of diffusion](https://www.evo-lite.com/stories/science-of-diffusion)). Empirical support: at 60 LEDs/m (16.7 mm pitch) a deep channel nearly eliminates hotspots, and at 144 LEDs/m (6.9 mm pitch) "all the hotspots are gone" even in shallow channels ([The Hook Up, LED diffuser channel test](https://www.thesmarthomehookup.com/i-bought-every-led-diffuser-channel-on-amazon-to-find-the-best-one-mission-accomplished/)).

**What this means at 4 mm pitch.** The 60's assumed 4 mm pitch (unverified) is denser than a 144 LEDs/m strip. By the 1:1 rule of thumb, ~4 mm of clear space between LED die and diffuser inner face is enough for the diffuser to erase the emitters — and 34 mm of product height can afford 4 mm. This is the single biggest payoff of the dense-pitch decision: no light guide, no taper, no micro-lens work (the machinery of the [Google light-ring patents](https://patents.google.com/patent/US10914430B2/en)) is needed. A mild diffusion grade then suffices, which keeps transmission high (section 2).

**Secondary measures, all cheap:**
- White reflective surface behind and beside the LEDs, as on the Echo Dot's white PCB (printed circuit board) mask ([Microcontroller Tips teardown](https://www.microcontrollertips.com/teardown-inside-amazons-echo-dot/)) — recovers back-scattered light that the diffuser rejects.
- Side-firing LEDs exist for cases where depth truly isn't available — they emit parallel to the mounting plane into an edge-lit guide ([Butler Technologies, lighting design](https://butlertechnologies.com/blog/lighting-design)). For the 60 they would only complicate: a down-firing ring with 4 mm of standoff doesn't need them.
- The double-diffuser approach (two scattering surfaces separated by an air gap), which Philips uses on the Hue Play Gradient to kill residual point sources ([Hueblog](https://hueblog.com/2020/10/05/my-review-philips-hue-play-gradient-lightstrip/)) — worth having in reserve if the first prototype still shows scalloping: an inexpensive inner film plus the outer PMMA (acrylic) ring.
- Testing with mockups is the primary method; published rules only get you to a sane first prototype ([Evo-Lite](https://www.evo-lite.com/stories/science-of-diffusion)).

**One subtlety the datasheets flag:** heavy diffusion grades shift the correlated colour temperature of the light passing through — the Makrolon Lumen XT datasheet measures shifts up to a few hundred kelvin depending on grade and thickness ([PDS112 datasheet](https://www.eplastics.com/pdf/PDS112_Lumen.pdf)). For a ring whose colours *are* the interface (green = volume, amber = seek, white = the device, red = mute and recording), the white channel should be colour-tuned after the diffuser is chosen, not before.

## 4. Downward-firing light on light versus dark desks

The 60's ring is only visible as reflection off the desk, so the desk's reflectance is a multiplier the product does not control. Light reflectance value (LRV) figures from lighting practice: a reflective white surface sits around LRV 85 (near-white paint approaches the 90s), black surfaces around LRV 5 ([Warehouse-Lighting, light reflectance values](https://www.warehouse-lighting.com/blogs/lighting-resources-education/light-reflectance-values), [Resene, LRV and paint selection](https://www.resene.co.nz/homeown/problem-solver/light-reflectance-values-LRV.htm)); measured pine wood averages ~51 % ([Covet, LRV of timber cladding](https://www.covet.com.au/light-reflectance-values-lrv-facade-cladding)). So the same ring drive current produces roughly *seventeen times* the reflected luminance on white laminate as on a black desk mat (85/5 — arithmetic from the cited LRV figures, not a measured comparison). No fixed brightness can be right on both.

Surface finish matters as much as shade: on a glossy or glass desk a bare LED source produces distracting specular (mirror-like) reflections — the standard advice for desk lighting is to avoid placing point sources over gloss and to prefer matte surfaces ([Eureka Ergonomic, RGB lighting guidance](https://eurekaergonomic.com/blogs/eureka-ergonomic-blog/taming-rgb-distractions)). A glossy desk will show a sharp mirror image of the ring (and of any residual LED hotspots — gloss is the harshest possible test of section 3) alongside the diffuse glow. For ambient/bias lighting, 8–25 % of maximum brightness is the usual comfortable range ([Eureka Ergonomic, dark-room bias lighting](https://eurekaergonomic.com/blogs/eureka-ergonomic-blog/dark-room-editing-rgb-bias-color-accuracy)) — the ring needs headroom above that for the dark-desk case and a floor far below it for a white desk at night.

The precedents both solve this the same way: the Echo Dot carries an ambient light sensor and auto-dims its ring ([Predictable Designs teardown](https://predictabledesigns.com/product-development-teardown-of-an-amazon-echo-dot/)); the Nest Learning Thermostat dims its display by ambient light ([TechHive review](https://www.techhive.com/article/2442945/nest-learning-thermostat-4th-gen-review.html)). The 60 should do the same, plus a one-time user brightness calibration for the desk it lives on — the sensor sees room light, not desk reflectance.

Colour behaviour also shifts with the surface: the reflected glow takes on the desk's colour. On warm oak, the "white = the device" channel will read warm; on a dark green mat, red and green channels compress toward each other. This argues for keeping the four ownership colours (green, amber, white, red) maximally separated in hue and never encoding meaning in fine colour differences.

## 5. The 324° ring with the 36° gap — flaw or feature?

**Feature, with two conditions.** The reasoning:

1. **Full rings exist to serve 360° viewing; the 60 has a front.** The Echo's ring is full because a cylinder in the middle of a room is approached from any side, and early Echoes even used a ring segment to point at the person speaking. The HomePod's LED disc likewise faces the ceiling for all-round visibility ([iFixit HomePod teardown](https://www.ifixit.com/Teardown/HomePod+Teardown/103133)). The 60 is a desk instrument with a defined front and its sockets at the back; the gap sits precisely where no user looks. A full ring would spend money and engineering lighting the 42 mm of arc (36° at Ø135 — arithmetic from the stated geometry) that faces the wall.

2. **The gap is honest about the hardware.** The alternative — routing the diffuser over or around the USB-C and 3.5 mm sockets — produces exactly the failure the Google patents fight: non-uniform brightness where the light guide detours ([US10914430 and siblings](https://patents.google.com/patent/US10914430B2/en)). A deliberate, crisply-bounded gap is better executed than a full ring with a dim patch. The Xbox Series X shows the same logic in reverse: Microsoft faked a glow rather than engineer a real one badly ([Windows Central](https://www.windowscentral.com/xbox-series-x-looks-gorgeous-leds-we-dont-recommend-it)).

3. **A broken ring is an instrument; a full ring is a lamp.** Analogue gauges and meters sweep a partial arc with a defined start and end, and the 60's design language (machined dial, sixty detents, colour-as-value) is instrument language. If the ring ever displays a value as an arc — volume from 0 to max in green — a full circle has no anchor: 100 % and 0 % meet. The gap gives the sweep a natural zero at one end and a maximum at the other, and its position at the back makes the front dead-centre the 50 % point. (This is design reasoning, not a sourced claim.)

**The two conditions:**

- **The endpoints must be designed, not accidental.** The last LED before each end of the arc will otherwise read as a bright terminus smearing into the dark gap — the one place hot-spotting is guaranteed, because the diffuser is lit from one side only there. Either an opaque end-wall (baffle) at each end of the diffuser channel for a crisp, deliberate stop, or a software fade over the last few LEDs for a soft one. Pick one; the half-measure (bare diffuser end, full-brightness last LED) reads as a fault. (Rule-of-thumb reasoning from the diffusion behaviour in section 3; no published source treats arc termination directly.)
- **The gap must read as intentional at a glance.** 36° is 10 % of the circle — big enough to be clearly deliberate rather than a defect, which is the right side of the line. A smaller gap (say under ~15°) would risk reading as a dead segment. (Judgement, not sourced.)

At the assumed 4 mm pitch (unverified) on a ring of roughly Ø120–130 (unverified — depends where the LED ring sits inside Ø135), the 324° arc carries on the order of 85–95 LEDs — arithmetic from the stated assumptions. That is Hue-Gradient-class density on a fraction of the length, individually addressable colour zones included; sweeps, per-detent ticks and asymmetric effects are all available for free.

## Recommendations for the 60

1. **Diffuser: opal PMMA (acrylic) with beads through the body, 3 mm wall.** Start with PLEXIGLAS Satinice 0D010 DF — 83 % transmission at 3 mm with diffuser beads distributed through the material, the grade the manufacturer names for lamp covers ([Satinice datasheet 211-11](https://www.plexiglas.de/files/plexiglas-content/pdf/technische-informationen/211-11-EN-PLEXIGLAS-Satinice.pdf)); it is available as extruded tube Ø50–Ø200 mm × 3 mm wall, so a machined-from-tube ring is a real sourcing option for 60 units. If the 4 mm standoff of recommendation 2 still shows scalloping on a gloss test surface, step down to WH10 DC (70 %) rather than adding a second diffuser; only if both fail, add an inner diffusing film (the Hue double-diffuser pattern). Printed translucent PETG (the printable copolyester) is for bench-rig geometry iterations only — no published optical figures, and layer lines show in transmitted light ([Bambu Lab forum](https://forum.bambulab.com/t/led-diffuser-using-transparent-pc/185469)).

2. **Standoff: ≥4 mm clear distance from LED die to the diffuser's inner face, matching the 4 mm pitch (1:1 rule, [Ledrise](https://www.ledrise.eu/blog/led-pitch-and-distance-to-cover-for-optimum-backlighting/)); 6 mm if the height budget allows.** Paint the channel behind the LEDs white (white solder mask on the flex/PCB plus white channel walls, per the Echo Dot's reflector mask, [Microcontroller Tips](https://www.microcontrollertips.com/teardown-inside-amazons-echo-dot/)) to claw back the ~17–45 % the diffuser rejects. Verify uniformity on a gloss-black test plate — the worst case — before freezing the grade.

3. **Design the arc's endpoints and the brightness loop.** Opaque baffles at both ends of the 324° channel so the arc terminates crisply at the 36° gap, plus a software option to fade the last 2–3 LEDs (~8–12 mm of arc at the assumed pitch) for value-sweep animations. Add an ambient light sensor and a one-time desk calibration: reflected luminance differs by roughly 17× between a white desk (LRV ~85) and a black mat (LRV ~5) ([Warehouse-Lighting](https://www.warehouse-lighting.com/blogs/lighting-resources-education/light-reflectance-values)), so fixed brightness cannot be correct; bias-lighting practice puts comfortable ambient glow at 8–25 % of typical maximum ([Eureka Ergonomic](https://eurekaergonomic.com/blogs/eureka-ergonomic-blog/dark-room-editing-rgb-bias-color-accuracy)), and the ring needs range both far above (dark mat) and below (white desk at night) that band. Ring off must be a first-class state, as on the Sonos Era 100 ([Sonos support](https://support.sonos.com/en-us/article/enable-or-disable-the-status-light)).

## Possibly wrong

- **Nothing in the settled decisions is a mistake.** The downward-firing ring (Echo Dot 4th gen proved it at scale), the 324°/36° split (defensible as an instrument arc with the gap where no one looks), colour-only-from-light (the Xbox's painted glow shows the static alternative can't carry meaning), and the ~4 mm pitch (dense enough to make diffusion easy) all check out against the evidence gathered here.
- **One assumption to nail down: the desk is an uncontrolled optical component.** Not a design error, but the largest unquantified risk in the light story: the same ring will be near-invisible on a black matte mat and glaring on white laminate (LRV ~5 vs ~85, [sourced above](https://www.warehouse-lighting.com/blogs/lighting-resources-education/light-reflectance-values)), and glossy desks will mirror the ring and expose any residual hot-spotting ([Eureka Ergonomic on gloss surfaces](https://eurekaergonomic.com/blogs/eureka-ergonomic-blog/taming-rgb-distractions)). If the brightness-management loop (sensor + calibration) is not already in the requirements, it should be added as one, not left as firmware polish.
- **The 36° gap width should be checked against the actual socket cluster.** 36° at Ø135 is ~42 mm of arc (arithmetic from the stated geometry). USB-C and 3.5 mm receptacles plus clearance plausibly need less. If the connectors fit in, say, 30 mm, the gap is carrying ~12 mm of unexplained dark arc; the gap should be exactly as wide as its reason. Marked as a check, not a finding — connector layout is not in the material available to this research.
