# Section E — Human-interface research on rotary input

Research strand E of the industrial-design research set for the 60, the Ø135 mm desk dial by Cadrane. This document summarises published human-interface research on rotary controls: pointing-law comparisons, detent counts, menu-navigation cost, grip, the open-source SmartKnob community's experience with software-defined detents, and accessibility. Every figure carries a source link or is marked unverified. Several primary papers are paywalled; where only the abstract was readable, that is said.

---

## E1. Fitts's law: dials and knobs versus sliders and touch

Fitts's law is the speed–accuracy model of aimed movement: time to acquire a target grows with the log of (distance / target width). It was shown early on to apply to rotary movement, not just linear pointing. Crossman and Goodeve's classic analysis (circulated 1963, published 1983 in the Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology) modelled target-aimed hand movements as a series of corrective impulses and found the law held "for both translational hand and rotary wrist motions" ([Crossman & Goodeve 1983, DOI 10.1080/14640748308402133](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1080/14640748308402133); companion retrospective [Wing 1983](https://doi.org/10.1080/14640748308402132)). So a knob is a Fitts's-law device: doubling the angular distance to a target detent costs roughly one extra "bit" of movement time, and finer targets (closer detents) cost time too.

Direct three-way comparisons of knob vs slider vs touch are rare; the closest well-controlled studies come from cockpits and cars.

- **Aviation, Fitts's-law throughput.** Van Zon, Borst, Pool and Van Paassen (Human Factors, 2020) compared three flight-deck interfaces for navigation tasks in a simulator with 14 participants: the mode control panel (a physical panel of knobs and switches), the control display unit (a physical keypad-and-line-select unit), and a touchscreen navigation display. The touchscreen was the *least accurate* of the three; the mode control panel (the knob-based interface) had the *lowest throughput* (slowest); the control display unit was best on both counts, which the authors attribute to its tactile, physical nature ([journal page, paywalled — findings from the abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0018720819862146); [open ResearchGate copy](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/335156848_Touchscreens_for_Aircraft_Navigation_Tasks_Comparing_Accuracy_and_Throughput_of_Three_Flight_Deck_Interfaces_Using_Fitts'_Law)). The pattern to take away: touch is fast but inaccurate; knobs are accurate but slow across large distances.
- **Automotive, task time and glances.** An AutomotiveUI 2010 driving-simulator study (20 participants, infotainment/comfort/navigation/communication tasks) found the touchscreen consistently gave *shorter task times and lower error rates* than a remote rotary controller, and the rotary controller condition showed the worst lateral control and collision rates — the menu-heavy tasks punished the knob ([open PDF](https://www.auto-ui.org/10/adjunctproceedings/p13.pdf)).
- **Automotive, the opposite result for continuous tasks.** An SAE 2017 on-road study ("Can You Still Look Up? Remote Rotary Controller vs. Touchscreen", SAE paper 2017-01-1386) found a rotary controller met the NHTSA (the US National Highway Traffic Safety Administration) criterion of total eyes-off-road time ≤ 12 s for radio-tuning and sound-settings tasks while the touchscreen interface did not ([SAE abstract, paywalled](https://saemobilus.sae.org/papers/still-look-up-remote-rotary-controller-vs-touchscreen-2017-01-1386)). A 2026 review of in-vehicle touchscreen safety likewise reports that for climate-control tasks 41% of glances at a touchscreen exceeded 2 s versus 27% for physical controls, and concludes rotary controllers hold attention better for *continuous adjustment* tasks ([review, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2590198226000382)).
- **Finger touch itself is a noisy pointer.** Bi, Li and Zhai's FFitts-law work (CHI 2013 — CHI is the ACM human-factors-in-computing conference) showed plain Fitts's law under-models finger touch on small targets because the fingertip contact point is itself uncertain; an extra absolute-precision term is needed ([ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2470654.2466180); [open PDF](https://www3.cs.stonybrook.edu/~xiaojun/pdf/FFitts.pdf)).

**Reading for the 60:** the split in the literature is by task, not by device religion. For *discrete selection among many visible targets*, touch wins on speed. For *continuous, magnitude-style adjustment* (volume, seek) and for anything done without looking, the knob wins on accuracy and glance economy. The 60 has both surfaces; the research says route each task to the surface that wins it.

Also relevant to feel: Knowles and Sheridan's 1966 study "The 'Feel' of Rotary Controls: Friction and Inertia" (Human Factors) measured detection thresholds for changes in a knob's friction and inertia at about 10–20% of the starting value, and found users *prefer more inertia and less friction*, and prefer viscous (speed-proportional) friction over stick-slip friction ([SAGE abstract, paywalled](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/001872086600800303); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/5965873/)). A heavy machined ring with good bearings is, per this 60-year-old result, the preferred feel — mass is a feature.

## E2. Detent count versus task

There is no single peer-reviewed "detents-per-revolution dose–response" study; the evidence is a triangulation of component practice, standards-era switch design, and haptics research.

- **Component practice.** Commodity mechanical encoders for human interfaces cluster at 12–30 detents per revolution; guides distinguish coarse-click parts (12–16 detents/rev, fast low-effort navigation) from fine-click parts (24–30 detents/rev, precise adjustment) ([Bourns encoder blog](https://bourns.blog/2020/07/28/new-rotary-encoder-model-features-high-detent-force-with-smooth-tactile-feedback/); [Alibaba engineering buying guide on 30-detent encoders](https://electronics.alibaba.com/buyingguides/30-pulse-30-detent-rotary-encoder-guide); [EEVblog thread on pulses vs detents](https://www.eevblog.com/forum/beginners/rotary-encoders-pulses-and-detents-per-revolution/)). The 60's physical pitch of 6° (sixty detents per revolution) is at the fine end of this range — a "precision adjustment" pitch, not a "flick between five modes" pitch.
- **Selector-switch practice.** Discrete rotary selector switches — where each detent is a *named position* rather than an increment — conventionally use 30° or 45° between positions (i.e. 8–12 positions per revolution); 30° spacing is described as standard for 12-position switches, and sub-15° spacing is noted as too small for a positive detent action in mechanical switch patents ([RS Components rotary-switch guide](https://uk.rs-online.com/web/content/discovery/ideas-and-advice/rotary-switches-guide); [rotary switch patent US4348569 discussing 15° spacing limits](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/4348569)). This is component practice, not a human-performance study, but it encodes decades of the same lesson: *mode selection wants coarse detents, adjustment wants fine ones.*
- **One detent = one item is the industry convention for menu scrolling.** Google's Android Automotive rotary-controller specification requires limitless rotation with detents, and hands the application "the number of detents" as the unit of focus movement — one click moves selection by one item ([Android Automotive rotary controller docs](https://source.android.com/docs/automotive/hmi/rotary_controller)).
- **Haptics research on detent perception.** Levesque et al. (CHI 2012, "Exploring the design space of programmable friction for scrolling interactions") ran five experiments on programmable detents for scrolling, covering the identifiability of distinct detent shapes, the *countability* of detents, and the perception of detent *density* ([ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/241628918_Exploring_the_design_space_of_programmable_friction_for_scrolling_interactions) — full text paywalled; findings above are from the abstract). Badescu, Wampler and Buttolo's "Rotary haptic knob for vehicular instrument controls" implemented detent ("hill") force fields that bias the knob toward item centres and away from item boundaries, precisely to make list selection self-centring ([open copy](https://www.academia.edu/14746813/Rotary_haptic_knob_for_vehicular_instrument_controls)). A 2024 study of force-feedback knobs for musical control found a detent mode significantly improved pitch accuracy versus no force feedback ([Actuators 13(11):462, DOI 10.3390/act13110462, open access](https://doi.org/10.3390/act13110462)).
- **Detents as confirmation, not speed.** The vehicular haptic-knob work above reports that feeling detents may not improve raw task time much, but gives the user confirmation and confidence — consistent with the SAE glance findings in section E1 (the value of detents shows up in *eyes-off-screen* metrics, not stopwatch metrics).

**What this means for 6 / 12 / 60 / 120:** 6 and 12 divisions (60°/30° effective spacing, made by the motor strengthening every fifth or tenth magnetic detent, or by counting detents in software) sit exactly in classic selector-switch territory and suit mode/menu selection. 60 (6°) suits continuous fine adjustment — volume — and matches fine-click encoder practice. 120 (3°) is *below* the pitch that mechanical practice considers viable for a positive detent, and would require the motor to interpose virtual detents between fixed magnetic ones; see the SmartKnob community's experience with fine virtual detents in section E5 before committing to it.

## E3. The cost of a click: menu navigation, steps-to-target, errors

- **The depth–breadth trade-off.** Miller's 1981 study — the seminal one — held total items at 64 and varied hierarchy shape from 64¹ (one flat menu) to 2⁶ (six binary levels). Acquisition speed and accuracy followed a U-shape; the best structure was **two levels of eight items**, and the fastest conditions also produced the fewest errors ([Miller 1981, Proceedings of the Human Factors Society](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/107118138102500179), paywalled — findings from the abstract and later reviews). Follow-up work confirmed that as depth and/or breadth grow past the optimum, reaction time, error rate and perceived complexity all rise ([Jacko & Salvendy, "Hierarchical Menu Design: Breadth, Depth, and Task Complexity"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/246367799_Hierarchical_Menu_Design_Breadth_Depth_and_Task_Complexity); [Landauer & Nachbar-era summary at UMD HCIL](https://www.cs.umd.edu/hcil/trs/99-15/99-15.html)).
- **In vehicles (the closest analogue to a glanceable desk device), breadth beats depth.** A modelling study of in-vehicle menu hierarchies found broader, shallower menus gave quicker acquisition than narrower, deeper ones ([ScienceDirect, paywalled abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0141938213000462)).
- **The rotary-specific cost.** With a knob, every item between the current position and the target is a physical click that must be traversed — steps-to-target is *linear* in list distance, where a touchscreen jump is one tap regardless of distance. This is exactly why the AutomotiveUI 2010 study in section E1 saw the rotary controller lose on menu-heavy tasks ([open PDF](https://www.auto-ui.org/10/adjunctproceedings/p13.pdf)). The mitigation used in practice is one detent per item plus self-centring haptic "hills" at item centres ([rotary haptic knob paper](https://www.academia.edu/14746813/Rotary_haptic_knob_for_vehicular_instrument_controls)).

**Arithmetic for the 60 (design reasoning, not a cited measurement):** with a ring menu selected at twelve o'clock, the worst-case rotation to any item in an N-item ring is N/2 clicks (the user can turn either way). A 6-item ring costs at most 3 clicks; a 12-item ring at most 6; a 60-item list at most 30, which is firmly in "scroll, don't select" territory. Miller's two-levels-of-eight optimum translates naturally to: two ring levels, 6–12 items each, never a third level.

## E4. Grip: fingertips, two fingers, palm — and a Ø135 mm rim

The grip literature is mostly about cylindrical handles and jar lids, which bracket the 60's use cases (the knurled ring is gripped at its rim, like a jar lid, because the top face is a touchscreen).

- **Power grip is optimal well below 135 mm.** Grip-strength studies find maximal grip force at a handle diameter around 18% of hand length (roughly 30–40 mm for adult hands) with force declining as diameter grows toward 80+ mm, because on a large diameter the fingers and thumb oppose each other instead of clamping against the palm ([handle diameter and hand–handle coupling](https://www.academia.edu/14983525/The_effect_of_torque_direction_and_cylindrical_handle_diameter_on_the_coupling_between_the_hand_and_a_cylindrical_handle); [effects of handle size and shape on grip strength](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/257036221_Effects_of_handle_size_and_shape_on_measured_grip_strength); [maximum-torque handle evaluation](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0169814105000855)). So nobody will *power-grip* the 60; that is fine, because turning it needs fractions of a newton-metre, not the several newton-metres a jar demands.
- **But torque capacity rises with diameter.** Jar-lid research shows achievable torque grows with lid diameter — one study measured minimum opening torques of 4.1 N·m at 65 mm and 6.6 N·m at 85 mm lid diameter as actually demanded by vacuum jars, against user strength distributions ([jar-opening demand vs wrist strength, ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0003687021000685)); a Dutch study of 750 adults over 50 recommended capping required opening torque at 2.0 N·m on a 66 mm lid ([the twisting force of aged consumers, ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S000368700100028X)); Crawford et al. found torque exertion rising with both lid diameter and lid *height* across their test range ([Ergonomics 2002 abstract](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00140130210162243)). A Ø135 mm rim therefore gives large mechanical advantage: even weak two-finger grips can produce far more torque than any detent will resist. Knurling and grip material measurably increase achievable torque on lids ([Nagashima & Konz 1986, "Jar Lids: Effect of Diameter, Gripping Materials and Knurling", paywalled abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/154193128603000713)) — the knurl earns its keep functionally, not just visually.
- **Grip styles on a rim.** For light precision turning, users use a fingertip/precision grip (thumb plus one or two fingers on the rim); heavier torque recruits more fingers and ultimately a whole-hand span. No published study of hand-resting posture on a ~135 mm desk puck was found in this pass — this is a genuine gap. Microsoft's design documentation for the Surface Dial (a Ø59 mm rotary desk puck — unverified diameter, from product materials) positions it as a *non-dominant-hand* device operated while the dominant hand does other work ([Microsoft Surface Dial interaction docs](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/apps/develop/input/windows-wheel-interactions); [fact sheet PDF](https://msftstories.thesourcemediaassets.com/2016/11/Surface-Dial-Fact-Sheet-Final.pdf)). Expect the 60 to be turned mostly by two or three fingertips of whichever hand is free, with the heel of the hand resting on the desk, not the device — worth verifying on the bench rig, since it is asserted here from product analogy, not a study.
- **A practical consequence of the ring-around-screen layout:** a full palm grip over the top (the natural way people turn large jar lids, per the lid studies above) would put the palm on the touchscreen. The rim grip is the only clean grip, and the touch stack needs palm/edge rejection for the moments users get it wrong. This is inference from geometry, not a cited finding.

## E5. The SmartKnob community: what software-defined detents are actually good for

SmartKnob View is Scott Bezek's open-source haptic input knob: a brushless gimbal motor with a magnetic encoder in closed-loop torque control, a round LCD in the centre, software-defined endstops and virtual detents ([GitHub repository](https://github.com/scottbez1/smartknob)). It is the closest existing thing to the 60's motor layer and its community is the best available field report.

What the community record says:

- **The headline caveat is cogging.** The project's own documentation warns that nearly every off-the-shelf brushless gimbal motor tested has moderate-to-severe cogging (torque ripple from the motor's own magnetics), so rotation is never fully smooth with detents off, and *the cogging can interfere with or overpower virtual detents when they are weak or finely spaced* ([SmartKnob GitHub README](https://github.com/scottbez1/smartknob); relayed in [CNX Software's coverage](https://www.cnx-software.com/2022/04/07/smartknob-view-is-a-diy-haptic-input-knob-with-wifi-and-bluetooth-connectivity/)). This is the single most load-bearing community finding for the 60: fine virtual detents are the first thing to turn to mush.
- **What people actually want it for:** the Hacker News discussion of the project surfaces recurring use cases — music/synthesizer parameter control (including wanting to *feel* named settings without looking), thermostats and smart-home dimming, and simulator controls (brake bias, fuel mapping) where one knob must serve many mappings ([Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=37448659)). The genuinely novel abilities cited are context-dependent feel (detent count, strength, endstops per mode) and snapping to a saved position or playing back an automation curve ([Hackaday, "Simplifying the SmartKnob", 2026](https://hackaday.com/2026/01/10/simplifying-the-smartknob/); [Hackaday in-depth look, 2022](https://hackaday.com/2022/06/24/an-in-depth-look-at-the-haptic-smart-knob/)).
- **Where it disappoints:** recurring community criticisms are complexity and reliability versus a plain potentiometer ("does one thing very well, reliably and cheap … can be controlled even with eyes closed"), continuous power draw to hold haptic state, size and cost that rule out embedded uses like a dimmer switch, and sourcing pain for low-cogging motors ([Hacker News thread](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=37448659); [Hackaday 2026 comments](https://hackaday.com/2026/01/10/simplifying-the-smartknob/)). One Hackaday commenter explicitly frames haptic feedback as an *accessibility* feature for interface navigation ([same 2026 article](https://hackaday.com/2026/01/10/simplifying-the-smartknob/)).
- **The 60's architecture already answers the two biggest complaints.** Passive magnetic detents that work unpowered remove the "haptics need power" objection, and the motor only *adds* dynamics on top rather than synthesising every click. This matches the layered reasoning in the 60's own dial-mechanism document (`docs/PREMIUM-BOM.md`: magnets own the fixed scale, motor owns dynamics, actuator owns event haptics) — the community evidence supports that split. The remaining transferred risk is exactly the fine-detent one: motor-made detents *between* the 6° magnetic poles (the 120-division mode) will be fighting the passive detent field the way SmartKnob's virtual detents fight cogging.

## E6. Accessibility: what a physical rotary offers that a touchscreen cannot

- **Flat glass gives no tactile ground truth.** Research on touchscreen access for blind and low-vision users starts from the observation that a touchscreen is a featureless surface offering no meaningful tactile stimulation, so all non-visual feedback must be synthesised (vibration, audio, electrostatics) ([University of Maine VEMI lab, touchscreen-based haptic information access](https://umaine.edu/vemi/resource/touchscreen-based-haptic-information-access-assisting-blind-visually-impaired-users-perceptual-parameters-design-guidelines/); [Investigating Touchscreen Accessibility for People with Visual Impairments](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/213801062_Investigating_Touchscreen_Accessibility_for_People_with_Visual_Impairments)). A knob with real detents is its own feedback channel: position and count are perceivable through the fingers with zero vision and zero sound — and on the 60, with zero power.
- **Tangible beats gesture in preference studies.** Kane et al.'s Touchplates work (ASSETS 2013 — ASSETS is the ACM conference on accessible computing) found visually impaired participants preferred tangible physical overlays on touchscreens to gesture-only interaction ([open PDF](https://faculty.washington.edu/wobbrock/pubs/assets-13.01.pdf)). A 2025/2026 study of a tangible user interface for older adults' smartphones reports good learnability and usability versus flat-glass interaction ([Universal Access in the Information Society, Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10209-025-01239-x)).
- **Motor impairment: touch demands fine pointing and steady tapping; a knob does not.** Studies of users with hand tremor show touchscreens fail them precisely at fine pointing and steady taps on small targets ([W4A 2015, reducing fine pointing and steady tapping for tremor, ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2745555.2747277); [touch-screen performance with and without motor-control disabilities, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23021630/)). A detented knob converts target acquisition into a count of discrete clicks with a self-centring resting position between them — the haptic "hill" effect in the vehicular haptic-knob literature is exactly a tremor-tolerant selection mechanism ([rotary haptic knob for vehicular instrument controls](https://www.academia.edu/14746813/Rotary_haptic_knob_for_vehicular_instrument_controls)). Recent work is evaluating infotainment rotary controllers specifically for drivers with disabilities ([Springer chapter, 2026 proceedings, paywalled](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-032-30552-7_37) — cited here for existence of the research direction, not for findings, which were not readable).
- **Age.** Older adults show measurably reduced hand-rotation control versus younger adults ([comparative study, PMC open access](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12322605/)), and jar-lid work (section E4) shows their torque capacity is the limiting distribution — so detent torque on the 60 should be validated against older/weaker hands, not the designer's.
- **One caution that cuts the other way:** the 60's screen language uses a *single green dot* as the selection indicator. Colour used as the only signal is an accessibility failure mode (red–green colour-vision deficiency affects roughly 8% of men — unverified commonly cited figure; the design principle, "don't use colour alone", is [WCAG — the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines — success criterion 1.4.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/use-of-color.html)). The dot's fixed twelve-o'clock *position* is itself a second, non-colour cue, which largely answers this — as long as position, not colour, is what actually distinguishes selected from unselected items.

## Recommendations for the 60

1. **Map divisions to tasks as: 6 or 12 detents per revolution for menu/mode selection, 60 for continuous adjustment (volume, seek), and treat 120 as experimental until the bench rig proves it.** Selector-switch practice puts named-position detents at 30–45° spacing ([RS rotary-switch guide](https://uk.rs-online.com/web/content/discovery/ideas-and-advice/rotary-switches-guide)), fine-click encoder practice puts adjustment detents at 24–30+ per revolution ([Bourns](https://bourns.blog/2020/07/28/new-rotary-encoder-model-features-high-detent-force-with-smooth-tactile-feedback/)), and the SmartKnob community's documented experience is that weak or finely spaced virtual detents get overpowered by the motor's own passive torque field ([SmartKnob README](https://github.com/scottbez1/smartknob)) — on the 60 the fixed 6° magnetic field plays the role of cogging against any 3° motor-interposed detent. Use one detent per menu item, per the automotive convention ([Android Automotive rotary spec](https://source.android.com/docs/automotive/hmi/rotary_controller)), with the motor deepening the "hill" at item centres ([rotary haptic knob paper](https://www.academia.edu/14746813/Rotary_haptic_knob_for_vehicular_instrument_controls)).
2. **Keep ring menus to at most two levels of 6–12 items.** Miller's depth–breadth result (optimum: two levels of eight items, with the fastest structures also the least error-prone — [Miller 1981](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/107118138102500179)) and the in-vehicle finding that breadth beats depth ([menu-hierarchy modelling study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0141938213000462)) both fit the dial's geometry: a 12-item ring is at most 6 clicks from anything. Anything longer than ~12 items should scroll under the fixed twelve-o'clock dot rather than be laid out as selectable ring positions.
3. **Route discrete jumps to touch, continuous and eyes-free actions to the ring — deliberately, per task.** The automotive evidence splits cleanly: touch wins menu-heavy discrete tasks on time and errors ([AutomotiveUI 2010](https://www.auto-ui.org/10/adjunctproceedings/p13.pdf)), the rotary controller wins continuous adjustment on glance economy and was the only one to meet the NHTSA 12-second eyes-off-road criterion ([SAE 2017-01-1386](https://saemobilus.sae.org/papers/still-look-up-remote-rotary-controller-vs-touchscreen-2017-01-1386); [2026 touchscreen-safety review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2590198226000382)). Never force a long list traversal through rotation alone when a tap could jump there.

## Possibly wrong

1. **The 120-step division.** The product context presents 120 as an even division the sixty detents support. Physically it is the one division the passive detents *oppose*: every second step of a 120-division mode falls at the energy maximum between two magnetic poles. The SmartKnob project's own documentation says fine or weak virtual detents are overpowered by an underlying passive torque field (there, motor cogging — [SmartKnob README](https://github.com/scottbez1/smartknob)), and mechanical-switch practice treats sub-15° spacing as too fine for positive detent action ([US patent 4348569 discussion](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/4348569)). 120 may only be honest as a *software* resolution (half-steps counted between clicks), not a *felt* one. The bench rig should test whether a motor-made 3° detent between 6° magnetic poles is perceivable at all.
2. **A single green dot as the selection indicator** relies on colour salience; if colour ever becomes the *only* difference between selected and unselected states it fails the don't-use-colour-alone principle ([WCAG 1.4.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/use-of-color.html)). The fixed twelve-o'clock position is a sufficient second cue only if unselected items never occupy that position ambiguously.
3. **An implicit grip assumption.** Jar-lid research (the closest literature to a Ø135 mm knurled rim) is all about grips that use the lid's *top face* as well as its rim — on the 60 the top face is the touchscreen, so the only clean grip is a rim grip, and accidental palm/finger contact with the glass during energetic turning is likely. No study of hand posture on a ~135 mm rotating desk puck was found; this needs empirical checking, and the touch stack needs edge/palm rejection regardless. (Gap stated in section E4; the rim-grip consequence is inference from geometry, not a cited finding.)
