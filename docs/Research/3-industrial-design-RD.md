# R&D brief — industrial design references for the 60

**You are being handed one research strand of a hardware product.** You have no other
context; everything you need is here. This is design research, not styling:
bring evidence about *why* the references work, with sources and images where
you can link them.

## The product

The 60, by Cadrane, is a desk dial: a Ø135 mm round touchscreen puck with a
heavy knurled metal ring — the knob — around the screen, on a steel base, 34 mm
tall. The knob is three quarters of the visible side; below it you see only the
edge of the steel plate and a thin ring of light that fires down onto the desk.
The knob turns on wheels and clicks through sixty magnetic detents that work with
the power off; a motor adds end-stops and dynamic feel on top. It controls a
computer's audio over USB-C. It is a heavy, expensive watch for the desk that
invites idle handling: people should pick it up and turn it for no reason.
Limited series; the customer buys KEF-LSX-class desk audio. Company name
Cadrane; product name the 60 (sixty detents). Category line: "a haptic desk dial".

Fixed physical facts: Ø135; 34 mm tall; the knob is CNC aluminium in production
(3D-printed for prototypes); a **wide 45° smooth chamfer** on the knob's top outer
edge is diamond-cut after anodising — one bright ring of raw aluminium against
matt black, the only jewellery on the object; a smaller smooth chamfer on the
inner edge where the ring meets the glass; a flat coin-edged rim between them; a
diamond knurl on the ring's side; a 1.2 mm chamfer on the ring's lower edge. The
base tapers slightly outward as it rises so the light ring looks down at the
desk. Sockets (USB-C, 3.5 mm) face away from the user at 12 o'clock. The screen
shows ring-shaped menus with a single green selection dot at twelve. Colour comes
only from light; the metal is monochrome. No visible screws, no logo on the
front.

## What is already decided — do not re-open

Diameter, height, the knob-dominant proportion, the chamfer-as-jewellery idea,
monochrome metal, light-only colour, rear-facing sockets, no battery, no visible
branding on the face. Your job is to make those decisions *better executed*, not
to reverse them.

## What is asked for

**A. The knurl and the turned edge.** Survey knurl patterns on objects people
find satisfying to handle and why: watch crowns and bezels (Rolex Submariner,
Tudor, Hublot Big Bang, Panerai, IWC, Sinn — count the teeth, the depth, the
pitch, the profile), camera lens rings (Leica, Fuji, Zeiss), tactical torches
(Surefire, the "Hexxagon"), pens (Rotring 600, Kaweco), audio knobs (Naim,
McIntosh, Chord, the KEF LSX controls), Teenage Engineering, Apple's Digital
Crown. Pitch and depth versus diameter; straight vs diamond vs "clous de Paris";
what pitch reads as precise vs aggressive; how the transition from knurl to a
polished chamfer is handled on the best examples. Give recommended numbers for a
Ø134 aluminium ring turned with fingertips.

**B. Proportion and stance.** Objects that sit on a desk and read as
permanent rather than portable: the HomePod mini, Nest Thermostat (the original
Tony Fadell one), the Jabra Speak2, Braun's Dieter Rams objects (T3, the SK4
controls, ET66), Bang & Olufsen (Beosound Balance, Beoplay A9 knob), Devialet
Phantom, KEF LSX and LS50, Naim Mu-so's dial, the Marshall and Ruark dials,
Leica's M-series top plates, the Apple Watch Ultra crown guard. For each what
the height-to-diameter ratio is, how the object meets the desk (foot, plinth,
shadow gap, chamfer), how mass is signalled. Say what our 0.25 ratio and the
2.8 mm shadow line under the knob will read as, and what would improve it.

**C. Automotive rotary controllers as feel references.** BMW iDrive (the
glass-topped later ones), Audi MMI, Mercedes COMAND, Porsche PCM, Bentley and
Rolls-Royce rotary and organ-stop controls, Volvo's Orrefors crystal shifter,
the Genesis crystal-sphere controller. What their detents feel like, how many,
how the surface finish invites the hand, how they light up, and what has been
written about them by reviewers and in Car Design News.

**D. Light as the only colour.** Under-glow and halo lighting done well: the Nest
Thermostat, the Xbox Series X vent, Nanoleaf, Philips Hue Play, the Sonos Era,
the light rings on Echo and HomePod, Dyson, Teenage Engineering's OB-4, high-end
keyboards. Diffuser materials and geometry (opal PMMA vs printed translucent PETG
vs frosted PC), how they avoid hot-spotting from discrete LEDs at 4 mm pitch, how
a downward-firing ring reads on light and dark desks, and what a 324° ring with a
36° opaque gap at the back looks like — is the gap a flaw or a feature?

**E. Human-interface research.** The literature on rotary input: Fitts'-law
studies of dials vs sliders vs touch, detent count vs task (the 60 divides into
6, 12, 60, 120), the "cost of a click" in menu navigation, one-handed vs
two-finger vs palm turning, how people rest a hand on a 135 mm object, and the
findings from the SmartKnob community about what dynamic detents are good for.
Include accessibility: what a rotary controller offers people with low vision or
motor impairment that a touchscreen does not.

**F. Packaging, unboxing and finish.** Since the object is sold as a watch-like
possession: how Leica, Bang & Olufsen, Teenage Engineering and the watch houses
handle the box, the certificate, the cable, the manual. Anodising and
diamond-cutting suppliers and what finishes (matt black, dark grey, natural)
survive daily fingertip contact best.

## Form of the answer

One document with images linked. Findings by section, each ending with two or
three concrete recommendations for the 60 with numbers where numbers apply
(knurl pitch, chamfer widths, shadow-line height, diffuser material). A short
list of things you think we have got wrong, with the evidence, kept separate
from the rest.
